### Introduction

Look around the room where you are sitting. You probably see a computer, a desk, a window, a phone, and a cup. In English, these are all inanimate things, universally referred to as "it." In French, however, every single physical object and abstract concept belongs to one of two grammatical categories: masculine or feminine. A table (*la table*) is feminine, while a desk (*le bureau*) is masculine. A book (*le livre*) is masculine, while a chair (*la chaise*) is feminine.

For adult English speakers, grammatical gender often feels arbitrary and intimidating. But mastering noun gender and articles is not an optional aesthetic choice; it is the structural backbone of French communication. In the TCF Canada exam, gender controls the choice of articles (*un*, *une*, *le*, *la*), possessive forms (*mon*, *ma*), and adjective agreements. In the first eight questions of the *Compréhension orale* (Listening) section, candidates frequently must identify pictures of common objects based on short spoken announcements. Using the wrong gender immediately signals hesitation and lowers your grammatical accuracy score across both Speaking and Writing.

In this lesson, you will demystify French grammatical gender. You will master both definite articles (*the*) and indefinite articles (*a / an / some*), learn high-frequency vocabulary for workplace and domestic objects, discover reliable spelling patterns that predict gender with over 85% accuracy, and learn how to describe items using color adjectives with proper agreement.

### The concept of grammatical gender in French

In French, there is no neuter pronoun like "it." Every noun is inherently either **masculine** (*le masculin*) or **feminine** (*le féminin*). 

This gender has nothing to do with biological sex or physical properties. A necktie (*la cravate*) is feminine though traditionally worn by men, while a suit (*un costume*) is masculine. The word for person (*la personne*) is always feminine, even when referring to a man, while the word for mannequin (*le mannequin*) is masculine.

Because gender is an inseparable property of each noun, you must adopt the golden habit of language acquisition: **never memorize a French noun alone.** Always learn every noun accompanied by its article:

> ✗ Never learn: *ordinateur* (computer)  
> ✓ Always learn: **un ordinateur** (/œ̃.n‿ɔʁ.di.na.tœʁ/) or **l'ordinateur** (masculine)

> ✗ Never learn: *chaise* (chair)  
> ✓ Always learn: **une chaise** (/yn ʃɛz/) or **la chaise** (feminine)

Observe how native speakers introduce and identify objects:

> **C'est un livre intéressant.** — /sɛ.t‿œ̃ livʁ ɛ̃.te.ʁɛ.sɑ̃/  
> *It is an interesting book. (Masculine noun with "un")*

> **C'est une table moderne.** — /sɛ.t‿yn tabl mɔ.dɛʁn/  
> *It is a modern table. (Feminine noun with "une")*

> **Voici les clés du bureau.** — /vwa.si le kle dy by.ʁo/  
> *Here are the office keys. (Plural noun with "les")*

### Definite and indefinite articles

French articles tell the listener whether you are referring to a specific, known object (definite: "the") or an unspecific, general object (indefinite: "a / an / some").

#### 1. Indefinite Articles (Un, Une, Des)
Use indefinite articles when introducing an object for the first time, or when referring to one item among many (equivalent to English "a", "an", or plural "some"):

- **un** (/œ̃/) — placed before masculine singular nouns: *un stylo* (a pen)
- **une** (/yn/) — placed before feminine singular nouns: *une feuille* (a sheet of paper)
- **des** (/de/) — placed before all plural nouns: *des stylos* (some pens), *des feuilles* (some sheets)

Notice that French has a mandatory plural indefinite article: **des**. In English, you can say "I have pens" without any article. In French, you **cannot** say *"J'ai stylos"*; you must say **J'ai des stylos**.

#### 2. Definite Articles (Le, La, L', Les)
Use definite articles when referring to a specific item known to both speaker and listener, or when speaking about a category in general (equivalent to English "the"):

- **le** (/lə/) — before masculine singular nouns starting with a consonant: *le bureau* (the desk)
- **la** (/la/) — before feminine singular nouns starting with a consonant: *la porte* (the door)
- **l'** (/l/) — before any singular noun (masc. or fem.) starting with a **vowel** or silent **h**: *l'ordinateur* (the computer), *l'horloge* (the clock)
- **les** (/le/) — before all plural nouns: *les bureaux* (the desks), *les portes* (the doors)

| Category | Masculine Singular | Feminine Singular | Before Vowel / Silent H | All Plural Nouns |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Indefinite (a / some)** | **un** (/œ̃/) | **une** (/yn/) | **un** / **une** (with liaison) | **des** (/de/) |
| **Definite (the)** | **le** (/lə/) | **la** (/la/) | **l'** (/l/) | **les** (/le/) |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Phonetic Rule: The L' Elision

When *le* or *la* meets a noun starting with a vowel sound, the vowel of the article drops and is replaced by an apostrophe. This is called **elision**:
- *le + ordinateur* → **l'ordinateur** (/lɔʁ.di.na.tœʁ/)
- *la + adresse* → **l'adresse** (/la.dʁɛs/)
- *le + homme* → **l'homme** (/lɔm/)

When written with *l'*, the article does not tell you whether the noun is masculine or feminine! In your notes, always write **l'ordinateur (m.)** or **l'adresse (f.)** so you remember its gender when using adjectives or pronouns later.

</div>

#### Québec vs France

Everyday technology and automotive terms showcase notable vocabulary differences between Canada and France:
- An email: In Québec, the official term created by the OQLF is universally **un courriel** **(QC)** (blend of *courrier* + *électronique*). In France, you will hear *un e-mail* or *un mail*.
- A cellphone: In Québec, it is **un cellulaire** **(QC)**. In France, people say *un portable* or *un mobile*.
- A car: In formal contexts in both countries, it is **une voiture** or **un véhicule**. In casual Canadian speech, you will hear **un char** (/ʃaʁ/) **(QC)** used to mean a car (in European French, *un char* means a tank or chariot!).

On the TCF Canada, all of these terms appear in listening prompts and reading texts.

### Predicting gender from noun endings

While memorizing the article with each noun is essential, French noun endings (suffixes) provide powerful clues. Over 80% of French nouns follow regular suffix patterns:

#### Reliable Masculine Suffixes (over 90% masculine)
- **-ment**: *le gouvernement* (government), *le document* (document), *l'appartement* (apartment)
- **-age**: *le voyage* (trip), *le message* (message), *le garage* (garage) *(Exceptions: la plage, la cage, la page, l'image)*
- **-eau**: *le bureau* (office/desk), *le tableau* (board/painting), *le couteau* (knife)
- **-phone**: *le téléphone* (telephone), *le microphone* (microphone)
- **-isme**: *le réalisme* (realism), *le bilinguisme* (bilingualism)
- **-oir**: *le miroir* (mirror), *le couloir* (hallway), *le devoir* (homework)

#### Reliable Feminine Suffixes (over 90% feminine)
- **-tion / -sion**: *la nation* (nation), *la situation* (situation), *la décision* (decision)
- **-té / -tié**: *la société* (society), *la liberté* (freedom), *l'université* (university)
- **-ette**: *la bicyclette* (bicycle), *la disquette* (diskette), *la serviette* (towel/briefcase)
- **-ure**: *la voiture* (car), *la culture* (culture), *la nature* (nature)
- **-ance / -ence**: *la présence* (presence), *la différence* (difference), *la chance* (luck) *(Exception: le silence)*
- **-ode / -ade / -ude**: *la méthode* (method), *la salade* (salad), *l'attitude* (attitude)

When encountering a new vocabulary word in a TCF reading text, check the ending. If it ends in **-tion**, you can be 99% confident it is feminine and requires *la* or *une*.

### Describing objects with basic colors and agreement

In English, adjectives always precede the noun ("a blue pen"). In French, **most adjectives—including colors—are placed AFTER the noun**:

> **un stylo bleu** — /œ̃ sti.lo blø/  
> *a blue pen (Literal: a pen blue)*

> **une chaise verte** — /yn ʃɛz vɛʁt/  
> *a green chair (Literal: a chair green)*

Furthermore, color adjectives must **agree in gender and number** with the noun they describe:

1. **Regular colors add -e in the feminine**:
   - **bleu** (/blø/) → **bleue** (/blø/) — *blue* (spelling changes, pronunciation remains the same)
   - **vert** (/vɛʁ/) → **verte** (/vɛʁt/) — *green* (the silent *t* becomes audible /t/)
   - **gris** (/ɡʁi/) → **grise** (/ɡʁiz/) — *grey* (the silent *s* becomes audible /z/)
   - **noir** (/nwaʁ/) → **noire** (/nwaʁ/) — *black*

2. **Colors already ending in -e do not change in the feminine**:
   - **rouge** (/ʁuʒ/) → **rouge** (/ʁuʒ/) — *red*
   - **jaune** (/ʒon/) → **jaune** (/ʒon/) — *yellow*
   - **rose** (/ʁoz/) → **rose** (/ʁoz/) — *pink*

3. **Irregular feminine forms**:
   - **blanc** (/blɑ̃/) → **blanche** (/blɑ̃ʃ/) — *white*

4. **Invariable colors (never change, even in feminine or plural)**:
   - **orange** (/ɔ.ʁɑ̃ʒ/) — *orange*
   - **marron** (/ma.ʁɔ̃/) — *brown*

Observe these descriptions in complete sentences:

> **J'ai un ordinateur noir et une souris blanche.**  
> /ʒe œ̃.n‿ɔʁ.di.na.tœʁ nwaʁ e yn su.ʁi blɑ̃ʃ/  
> *I have a black computer and a white mouse.*

> **Le bureau a deux chaises rouges et une table grise.**  
> /lə by.ʁo a dø ʃɛz ʁuʒ e yn tabl ɡʁiz/  
> *The office has two red chairs and a grey table.*
