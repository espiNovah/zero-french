### Introduction

Family is one of the most universal topics of human conversation. When establishing connections in a new country or speaking with colleagues, sharing basic details about your family, your marital status, and the people in your life creates immediate rapport.

In the TCF Canada exam, family is a primary theme in **Task 1 of the Speaking test (*Entretien dirigé*)**. After you introduce your name and profession, the examiner may ask: *"Parlez-moi de votre famille"* ("Tell me about your family") or *"Vous avez des enfants ?"* ("Do you have children?"). In immigration applications and interviews with Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC), marital status and family composition are official legal categories that you must declare accurately.

In this lesson, you will learn the core vocabulary for immediate and extended family members, master the system of French **possessive adjectives** (*mon, ma, mes*), and learn how to describe your civil status using official Canadian immigration terminology.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Essential vocabulary for immediate family members and children.
- Extended family terms (*grands-parents, oncles, tantes, cousins*).
- How possessive adjectives work in French (*mon, ma, mes*, etc.) and how they agree with the noun possessed.
- The critical phonetic exception: using *mon* before feminine nouns starting with a vowel sound (*mon amie*).
- Expressing marital status: *célibataire, marié(e), divorcé(e)*, and the Canadian legal concept of *conjoint(e) de fait*.
- How to describe your family size using the verb *avoir* (*J'ai deux sœurs*).
- Common Canadian colloquial terms for partners (*mon chum*, *ma blonde*).

### Immediate family members

French nouns for family members carry grammatical gender (masculine or feminine). When referring to individuals, the article matches the biological or social gender of the person.

Observe how native speakers introduce their immediate family:

> **Voici mon père et ma mère.** — /vwa.si mɔ̃ pɛʁ e ma mɛʁ/  
> *Here is my father and my mother.*

> **J'ai un frère et deux sœurs.** — /ʒe œ̃ fʁɛʁ e dø sœʁ/  
> *I have one brother and two sisters.*

> **Nous avons un fils et une fille.** — /nu.z‿a.vɔ̃ œ̃ fis e yn fij/  
> *We have a son and a daughter.*

Notice the pronunciation of **fils** (son): the *l* is silent, and the final *s* is pronounced: `/fis/`. Do not pronounce it as *"feel"*. In contrast, **fille** (daughter or girl) is pronounced `/fij/`.

| Masculine Family Term | Feminine Family Term | Plural Collective Term | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **le père** (/lə pɛʁ/) | **la mère** (/la mɛʁ/) | **les parents** (/le pa.ʁɑ̃/) | father / mother / parents |
| **le fils** (/lə fis/) | **la fille** (/la fij/) | **les enfants** (/le.z‿ɑ̃.fɑ̃/) | son / daughter / children |
| **le frère** (/lə fʁɛʁ/) | **la sœur** (/la sœʁ/) | **les frères et sœurs** | brother / sister / siblings |
| **le mari** (/lə ma.ʁi/) | **la femme** (/la fam/) | **le couple** (/lə kupl/) | husband / wife / couple |
| **l'époux** (/le.pu/) | **l'épouse** (/le.puz/) | **les époux** (/le.z‿e.pu/) | spouse (masc.) / spouse (fem.) |

> **Parents vs Relatives**: In French, **mes parents** usually means your *father and mother*, but **un parent** can also mean any relative (*un parent éloigné* = a distant relative). For "relatives" as a group, you can say **la famille élargie**, or in Québec **la parenté** **(QC)**. **Les proches** means the people close to you (family and close friends).

### Possessive adjectives: Agreement with the possessed noun

In English, possessive adjectives reflect the **gender of the owner**: "his mother" (because he is male), "her father" (because she is female).

In French, possessive adjectives obey a completely different rule: **they agree in gender and number with the noun that is possessed, NOT with the owner.**

Observe this fundamental contrast:

> **David parle de sa mère.** — /da.vid paʁl də sa mɛʁ/  
> *David is talking about his mother. ("mère" is feminine, so we use "sa", even though David is a man!)*

> **Sophie parle de son père.** — /sɔ.fi paʁl də sɔ̃ pɛʁ/  
> *Sophie is talking about her father. ("père" is masculine, so we use "son", even though Sophie is a woman!)*

> **Pierre et Marie aiment leurs enfants.** — /pjɛʁ e ma.ʁi ɛm lœʁ.z‿ɑ̃.fɑ̃/  
> *Pierre and Marie love their children. ("enfants" is plural, so we use "leurs")*

#### The Complete Possessive Adjective Matrix

| Owner | Masculine Singular Noun | Feminine Singular Noun | Plural Nouns (Masc. & Fem.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **I (Je)** | **mon** (/mɔ̃/) | **ma** (/ma/) | **mes** (/me/) |
| **You informal (Tu)** | **ton** (/tɔ̃/) | **ta** (/ta/) | **tes** (/te/) |
| **He / She / It (Il/Elle)**| **son** (/sɔ̃/) | **sa** (/sa/) | **ses** (/se/) |
| **We (Nous)** | **notre** (/nɔtʁ/) | **notre** (/nɔtʁ/) | **nos** (/no/) |
| **You formal/plural (Vous)**| **votre** (/vɔtʁ/) | **votre** (/vɔtʁ/) | **vos** (/vo/) |
| **They (Ils/Elles)** | **leur** (/lœʁ/) | **leur** (/lœʁ/) | **leurs** (/lœʁ/) |

Notice that for *nous*, *vous*, and *ils/elles*, there is no gender distinction in the singular: you use **notre**, **votre**, and **leur** for both masculine and feminine singular nouns.

### The vowel rule: Mon, ton, son before feminine nouns

There is a vital phonetic rule in French that every beginner must internalize: **French avoids having two vowel sounds clash directly together (hiatus).**

If a feminine noun begins with a vowel sound or a silent *h*, using *ma*, *ta*, or *sa* would cause an awkward pause (*ma... amie*). To ensure a smooth liaison, French replaces *ma*, *ta*, *sa* with the masculine forms **mon**, **ton**, **son**.

Compare these pairs:

> **mon amie** (/mɔ̃.n‿a.mi/) — *my (female) friend*  
> ✗ *ma amie* (Impossible in French)

> **son épouse** (/sɔ̃.n‿e.puz/) — *his/her wife*  
> ✗ *sa épouse* (Impossible in French)

> **ton histoire** (/tɔ̃.n‿is.twaʁ/) — *your story / history*  
> ✗ *ta histoire* (Impossible in French: silent "h" acts like a vowel)

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Grammar Trap

Whenever you see a noun that you know is feminine (like *amie*, *école*, *équipe*, *adresse*), do not automatically write *ma*. Check the first letter! If it begins with a vowel (*a, e, i, o, u, y*) or silent *h*, it **must** take **mon**, **ton**, or **son**:
- **mon école** (*my school*, fem.)
- **son adresse** (*his/her address*, fem.)
- **mon idée** (*my idea*, fem.)

</div>

### Civil status and Canadian immigration terminology

In everyday life and particularly in Canadian immigration forms (such as Express Entry, Provincial Nominee Programs, and CSQ for Québec), civil status is an essential category.

Observe how speakers state their relationship status using the verb **être**:

> **Je suis marié depuis cinq ans.** — /ʒə sɥi ma.ʁje də.pɥi sɛ̃.k‿ɑ̃/  
> *I have been married for five years. (Masculine speaker)*

> **Je suis mariée et nous avons deux enfants.** — /ʒə sɥi ma.ʁje e nu.z‿a.vɔ̃ dø.z‿ɑ̃.fɑ̃/  
> *I am married and we have two children. (Feminine speaker: note the extra "e")*

> **Nous sommes conjoints de fait.** — /nu sɔm kɔ̃.ʒwɛ̃ də fɛ/  
> *We are common-law partners.*

| Civil Status Term | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **célibataire** | /se.li.ba.tɛʁ/ | single | Same form for masc. and fem. |
| **marié / mariée** | /ma.ʁje/ | married | Add *-e* for feminine (sound is identical) |
| **conjoint de fait** | /kɔ̃.ʒwɛ̃ də fɛ/ | common-law partner (male) | Official Canadian legal status (cohabitation ≥ 1 yr) |
| **conjointe de fait** | /kɔ̃.ʒwɛ̃t də fɛ/ | common-law partner (female) | Feminine form adds audible /t/ sound |
| **divorcé / divorcée** | /di.vɔʁ.se/ | divorced | Add *-e* for feminine |
| **séparé / séparée** | /se.pa.ʁe/ | separated | Add *-e* for feminine |
| **veuf / veuve** | /vœf / vœv/ | widower / widow | Irregular feminine spelling and pronunciation shift |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In France, people commonly introduce their partner casually as **mon copain / ma copine** (for boyfriend/girlfriend) or **mon compagnon / ma compagne** (for long-term unmarried partners). In Québec, you will almost universally hear **mon chum** (/tʃɔm/) **(QC)** for boyfriend/male partner and **ma blonde** (/blɔ̃d/) **(QC)** for girlfriend/female partner, regardless of hair color! On the TCF Canada exam, use the neutral standard terms: **mon conjoint / ma conjointe** or **mon mari / ma femme**.

</div>

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--warning" markdown="1">

#### Exam tip

In Speaking Task 1, if the examiner asks about your family, keep your answer structured, clear, and positive:
1. State your marital status (*Je suis marié / célibataire*).
2. Mention your partner or children if applicable (*J'ai une fille de trois ans*).
3. Mention where your immediate family lives (*Mes parents habitent en Algérie, mais mon frère habite à Toronto*).
Using simple connectors like **mais** (*but*), **et** (*and*), and **avec** (*with*) demonstrates A2-level communicative organization.
