### Introduction

Greeting someone properly is the universal starting point for any French interaction. In the Francophone world, social encounters do not begin by jumping straight into business or asking a transaction question; they begin with an explicit ritual of acknowledgement. Entering a bakery in Montréal or walking into an office in Paris without saying hello is perceived not just as abrupt, but as actively disrespectful.

For the TCF Canada candidate, greetings carry immediate high-stakes implications. In the *Expression orale* (Speaking) examination, the moment you walk into the room, your interaction with the examiner has begun. The examiner hears how you handle register from your first word. Choosing the wrong register—addressing an examiner as *tu* instead of *vous*, forgetting a greeting formula, or omitting basic politeness markers—creates a poor impression and can weigh on your overall speaking score.

In this lesson, you will master the foundational vocabulary and social mechanics of French greetings, farewells, and polite exchanges. You will learn to navigate the critical distinction between *tu* (informal) and *vous* (formal), understand Canadian versus European French variations, and practice real spoken cadences to build immediate conversational confidence.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The fundamental social divide between *tu* (informal) and *vous* (formal/plural).
- Time-based greetings (*bonjour*, *bonsoir*) and phone conventions (*allô*).
- How to ask and answer "How are you?" across different registers.
- Essential courtesy markers (*s'il vous plaît*, *merci*, *de rien*, *excusez-moi*).
- Farewell formulas for immediate departures, future meetings, and end-of-day wishes.
- Phonetic keys for nasal vowels (/ɔ̃/, /ɑ̃/) and polite liaison links.
- Key differences between European French and Québec French everyday etiquette.

### Greetings by time of day

In French, your choice of greeting depends primarily on two factors: the time of day and the degree of social distance between you and the other person. Unlike English, where "hello" or "hi" serves in almost any situation, French maintains clear boundaries.

Observe how native speakers greet each other across different hours:

> **Bonjour, madame.** — /bɔ̃.ʒuʁ, ma.dam/ (bohn-ZHOOR, mah-DAHM)  
> *Good morning / Hello, ma'am.* (Used from morning until dusk)

> **Bonsoir, monsieur.** — /bɔ̃.swaʁ, mə.sjø/ (bohn-SWAHR, muh-SYEUH)  
> *Good evening, sir.* (Used starting around 5:00 or 6:00 pm)

> **Salut, Thomas !** — /sa.ly, tɔ.ma/ (sah-LEW, toh-MAH)  
> *Hi, Thomas!* (Casual greeting strictly reserved for friends, peers, and children)

The standard universal daytime greeting is **bonjour**. It is a compound word formed from *bon* ("good") and *jour* ("day"). You use it from early morning until late afternoon (around 5:00 pm or 6:00 pm). Once daylight begins to fade or evening activities commence, speakers switch naturally to **bonsoir** (*bon* + *soir*, "good evening"). Using *bonjour* at 8:00 pm sounds noticeably foreign to native ears.

Notice that **salut** means both "hi" and informal "bye". It is an informal greeting used among friends, colleagues of equal status who already know each other well, young people, and family members. Never use *salut* when addressing an immigration official, a store clerk you do not know, a doctor, or an elder.

When answering the telephone, French speakers typically start with the telephone marker *allô*, very often followed by *bonjour*:

> **Allô ? Oui, bonjour.** — /a.lo ? wi, bɔ̃.ʒuʁ/ (ah-LOH ? WEE, bohn-ZHOOR)  
> *Hello? Yes, hello.*

| French | IPA | Phonetic Respelling | English Translation | Register & Usage Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bonjour** | /bɔ̃.ʒuʁ/ | **bohn-ZHOOR** | Hello / Good day | Universal (formal and polite neutral); used until evening |
| **Bonsoir** | /bɔ̃.swaʁ/ | **bohn-SWAHR** | Good evening | Universal (formal and polite neutral); used from ~5:30 pm onwards |
| **Salut** | /sa.ly/ | **sah-LEW** | Hi / Hey | Strictly informal; close friends, peers, and children |
| **Allô** | /a.lo/ | **ah-LOH** | Hello | Mainly on the telephone (*Allô, bonjour* is normal); in Québec, also an informal in-person greeting |

### Tu vs Vous: The socio-cultural divide

English uses the single pronoun "you" whether addressing a toddler, your best friend, your boss, or the Prime Minister of Canada. French makes a profound grammatical and social distinction between two distinct forms of address: **tu** and **vous**.

Compare these paired examples to observe how verb endings and pronouns change:

> **Tu vas bien ?** — /ty va bjɛ̃/ (tew vah BYEN)  
> *Are you doing well?* (Informal address to a friend)

> **Vous allez bien ?** — /vu.z‿a.le bjɛ̃/ (voo-zah-lay BYEN)  
> *Are you doing well?* (Formal address to an examiner or customer)

The distinction between these two pronouns follows consistent social guidelines:

1. **Tu (le tutoiement)**:
   - Used when speaking to a single person with whom you share familiarity.
   - Reserved for family members, close friends, romantic partners, classmates, children under roughly 15 years old, and pets.
   - In adult professional environments, coworkers often transition to *tu* over time, but only after an explicit mutual agreement.

2. **Vous (le vouvoiement)**:
   - **Singular formal**: Used when speaking to any single adult whom you do not know well, including store clerks, bus drivers, doctors, colleagues, superiors, and administrative officials.
   - **Plural**: *Vous* is also the mandatory plural "you" (like "you all" or "y'all") whenever you address two or more people, regardless of whether you would use *tu* with them individually.

The golden rule for adult immigrants is simple: **when in doubt, always use vous**. Addressing someone as *vous* signals respect and professional courtesy. If the other person prefers a more casual relationship, they will invite you to switch by saying:

> **On peut se tutoyer.** — /ɔ̃ pø sə ty.twa.je/ (ohn peuh suh tew-twah-YAY)  
> *We can use "tu" with each other.*

Unless the prompt card makes the other person a friend, using *tu* during this roleplay is a register error that weakens your performance.

### Asking how someone is doing

In everyday Francophone life, a greeting is almost always paired with an inquiry about the other person's well-being. Notice how the phrasing adjusts to match the register (*tu* vs *vous*).

Observe the patterns below:

> **Comment allez-vous ?** — /kɔ.mɑ̃.t‿a.le vu/ (koh-mahn-tah-lay VOO)  
> *How are you?* (Formal, using subject-verb inversion and liaison)

> **Comment vas-tu ?** — /kɔ.mɑ̃ va ty/ (koh-mahn vah TEW)  
> *How are you?* (Informal standard)

> **Ça va ?** — /sa va/ (sah VAH)  
> *How's it going? / Everything okay?* (Neutral-to-informal, extremely common)

> **Comment ça va ?** — /kɔ.mɑ̃ sa va/ (koh-mahn sah VAH)  
> *How are things going?* (Neutral conversational)

Notice the phonetics of **Comment allez-vous ?**. Because the adverb *comment* ends in a consonant sound (/t/ in liaison) and *allez* begins with a vowel sound (/a/), the silent *t* links across words: `/kɔ.mɑ̃.t‿a.le vu/ (koh-mahn-tah-lay VOO)`. Similarly, the final *s* of *vous* links smoothly when followed by another vowel.

Here is a summary of common inquiries:

| French Inquiry | IPA | Phonetic Respelling | English Translation | When to Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Comment allez-vous ?** | /kɔ.mɑ̃.t‿a.le vu/ | **koh-mahn-tah-lay VOO** | How are you? | Formal; professional settings, elders, exam room |
| **Vous allez bien ?** | /vu.z‿a.le bjɛ̃/ | **voo-zah-lay BYEN** | Are you doing well? | Polite; slightly softer than formal inversion |
| **Comment vas-tu ?** | /kɔ.mɑ̃ va ty/ | **koh-mahn vah TEW** | How are you? | Informal; friends, peers, relatives |
| **Tu vas bien ?** | /ty va bjɛ̃/ | **tew vah BYEN** | Are you doing well? | Informal conversational |
| **Ça va ?** | /sa va/ | **sah VAH** | How's it going? | Universal informal / familiar everyday |
| **Comment ça va ?** | /kɔ.mɑ̃ sa va/ | **koh-mahn sah VAH** | How are things going? | Neutral friendly |

### Responding politely to greetings

When someone asks how you are doing, polite etiquette requires a three-step response:
1. State your condition (*bien*, *très bien*, *comme ci comme ça*).
2. Thank the speaker (*merci*).
3. Return the question (*et vous ?* or *et toi ?*).

Notice how these micro-dialogues flow naturally:

#### Dialogue A: Formal Exchange (Workplace or Exam Room)
> **Bonjour, monsieur. Comment allez-vous ?** (bohn-ZHOOR, muh-SYEUH. koh-mahn-tah-lay VOO ?)  
> *Hello, sir. How are you?*  
> **Très bien, merci. Et vous-même ?** (treh BYEN, mehr-SEE. ay voo-MEHM ?)  
> *Very well, thank you. And yourself?*  
> **Ça va bien, je vous remercie.** (sah vah BYEN, zhuh voo ruh-mehr-SEE.)  
> *I'm doing well, thank you.*

#### Dialogue B: Informal Exchange (Friends / Classmates)
> **Salut, Sarah ! Ça va ?** (sah-LEW, sah-RAH ! sah VAH ?)  
> *Hi, Sarah! How's it going?*  
> **Oui, ça va super, merci ! Et toi ?** (WEE, sah vah sew-PEHR, mehr-SEE ! ay TWAH ?)  
> *Yes, doing great, thanks! And you?*  
> **Pas mal, la routine !** (pah MAHL, lah roo-TEEN !)  
> *Not bad, the usual routine!*

Key response formulas:

| French Response | IPA | Phonetic Respelling | English Translation | Nuance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Très bien, merci.** | /tʁɛ bjɛ̃, mɛʁ.si/ | **treh BYEN, mehr-SEE** | Very well, thank you. | Standard polite response |
| **Ça va bien, merci.** | /sa va bjɛ̃, mɛʁ.si/ | **sah vah BYEN, mehr-SEE** | I'm doing well, thanks. | Standard neutral response |
| **Oui, ça va.** | /wi, sa va/ | **WEE, sah VAH** | Yes, I'm okay / fine. | Direct, casual confirmation |
| **Pas mal.** | /pa mal/ | **pah MAHL** | Not bad. | Modest, colloquial |
| **Comme ci, comme ça.** | /kɔm si, kɔm sa/ | **kohm SEE, kohm SAH** | So-so. | Expressing mediocre state |
| **Et vous ?** | /e vu/ | **ay VOO** | And you? (formal) | Returning the question formally |
| **Et toi ?** | /e twa/ | **ay TWAH** | And you? (informal) | Returning the question informally |

### Polite expressions and everyday etiquette

In French, courtesy words are not optional embellishments; they are mandatory structural anchors of polite speech. Omitting them makes sentences sound like blunt demands.

Observe how a request transforms when polite formulas are attached:

> **Un café, s'il vous plaît.** — /œ̃ ka.fe, sil vu plɛ/ (uhn kah-FAY, seel voo PLEH)  
> *A coffee, please.*

> **Merci beaucoup pour votre aide.** — /mɛʁ.si bo.ku puʁ vɔtʁ ɛd/ (mehr-SEE boh-KOO poor voht-ruh EHD)  
> *Thank you very much for your help.*

> **De rien, c'est avec plaisir.** — /də ʁjɛ̃, sɛ.t‿a.vɛk ple.ziʁ/ (duh RYEN, seh-tah-vek pleh-ZEER)  
> *You're welcome, it's my pleasure.*

Here are the essential courtesy formulas every candidate must master:

| French Phrase | IPA | Phonetic Respelling | English Meaning | Usage Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **S'il vous plaît** | /sil vu plɛ/ | **seel voo PLEH** | Please (formal) | To strangers, officials, clerks, examiners |
| **S'il te plaît** | /sil tə plɛ/ | **seel tuh PLEH** | Please (informal) | To friends, peers, children |
| **Merci** | /mɛʁ.si/ | **mehr-SEE** | Thank you | Universal gratitude marker |
| **Merci beaucoup** | /mɛʁ.si bo.ku/ | **mehr-see boh-KOO** | Thank you very much | Stronger expression of appreciation |
| **Je vous en prie** | /ʒə vu.z‿ɑ̃ pʁi/ | **zhuh voo-zahn PREE** | You're very welcome | Elevated, formal response to "merci" (essential for TCF) |
| **Je t'en prie** | /ʒə tɑ̃ pʁi/ | **zhuh tahn PREE** | You're welcome | Warm, informal response to "merci" (with friends/family) |
| **Avec plaisir** | /a.vɛk ple.ziʁ/ | **ah-vek pleh-ZEER** | With pleasure / My pleasure | Warm polite response |
| **De rien** | /də ʁjɛ̃/ | **duh RYEN** | You're welcome | Standard informal/neutral "don't mention it" |
| **Pas de problème** | /pa də pʁɔ.blɛm/ | **pah duh proh-BLEHM** | No problem | Everyday casual response |
| **Il n'y a pas de quoi** | /i.l‿n‿j‿a pa də kwa/ | **eel nyah pah duh KWAH** | Don't mention it | Often shortened to *Y'a pas de quoi* |
| **Excusez-moi** | /ɛk.sky.ze mwa/ | **ehk-skew-zay MWAH** | Excuse me (formal) | Getting someone's attention or apologizing |
| **Pardon** | /paʁ.dɔ̃/ | **pahr-DOHN** | Pardon / Sorry | Passing someone in a crowd, minor mistake |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In France, the universal reply to *merci* is **de rien** or **je vous en prie**. In Québec, you will often hear **bienvenue** **(QC)** used to mean "you're welcome" (modeled on English "welcome"). This is a familiar Québec usage that the OQLF advises against (it is a calque of English); the standard replies, in Québec as in France, are **de rien**, **je vous en prie** or **il n'y a pas de quoi**. In standard French, *bienvenue* means "welcome to a place" (*Bienvenue à Paris*). In the exam, use *de rien* or *je vous en prie*.

</div>

### Saying goodbye and wishing well

Ending a conversation in French involves more than a hasty "bye." It typically includes acknowledging departure and offering a warm wish for the remainder of the day or week.

Observe these common farewell formulas:

> **Au revoir, bonne journée !** — /o ʁə.vwaʁ, bɔn ʒuʁ.ne/ (oh ruh-VWAHR, buhn zhoor-NAY)  
> *Goodbye, have a good day!*

> **À bientôt, prenez soin de vous.** — /a bjɛ̃.to, pʁə.ne swɛ̃ də vu/ (ah byen-TOH, pruh-nay SWEN duh VOO)  
> *See you soon, take care of yourself.*

Notice the difference between **jour** (a point in time: *bonjour*) and **journée** (the duration of the day: *bonne journée*). Similarly, **soir** refers to the evening as a time point (*bonsoir*), whereas **soirée** denotes the duration of the evening (*bonne soirée*). Therefore, you say *bonjour* when arriving, but *bonne journée* ("have a good day") when leaving.

| Farewell Phrase | IPA | Phonetic Respelling | English Translation | Register & Tone |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Au revoir** | /o ʁə.vwaʁ/ | **oh ruh-VWAHR** | Goodbye | Universal standard; appropriate in every context |
| **Bonne journée** | /bɔn ʒuʁ.ne/ | **buhn zhoor-NAY** | Have a good day | Polite daytime departure |
| **Bonne soirée** | /bɔn swa.ʁe/ | **buhn swah-RAY** | Have a good evening | Polite evening departure |
| **Bonne nuit** | /bɔn nɥi/ | **buhn NWEE** | Good night | Exclusively before going to sleep |
| **À bientôt** | /a bjɛ̃.to/ | **ah byen-TOH** | See you soon | Neutral; implies seeing each other soon |
| **À demain** | /a də.mɛ̃/ | **ah duh-MEHN** | See you tomorrow | Specific to seeing someone the next day |
| **À tout à l'heure** | /a tu.t‿a lœʁ/ | **ah too-tah LEUHR** | See you in a little while | Same day, within a few hours |
| **Salut** | /sa.ly/ | **sah-LEW** | Bye | Informal; only with people you call *tu* |
| **Ciao** | /tʃa.o/ | **CHOW** | Bye | Colloquial, borrowed from Italian |

#### Exam tip

When concluding your TCF Canada Speaking examination (after Task 3), do not simply stand up in silence. Look the examiner in the eye, smile, and say: **"Merci beaucoup, bonne journée, au revoir."** Leaving with clear, confident polite formulas leaves a positive final impression on the examiner.

### Responding to polite situations: A reference table

To consolidate your mastery of registers, review this quick matrix of situational etiquette:

| Social Situation | Formal Address (*Vous*) | Informal Address (*Tu*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Entering a shop at 10:00 am | **Bonjour, madame / monsieur** | *(Not appropriate with strangers)* |
| Asking how someone is feeling | **Comment allez-vous ?** | **Ça va ? / Comment vas-tu ?** |
| Ordering a coffee / service | **Un café, s'il vous plaît** | **Donne-moi un café, s'il te plaît** |
| Thanking someone for assistance | **Merci beaucoup pour votre aide** | **Merci beaucoup pour ton aide** |
| Apologizing for bumping into someone | **Excusez-moi / Pardon** | **Pardon ! / Excuse-moi** |
| Departing an office at 4:30 pm | **Au revoir, bonne fin de journée** | **Salut, à plus !** |
