### Introduction

Every language journey begins with the alphabet, but in French, mastering the letters is far more than an academic exercise—it is a critical daily survival skill. As an immigrant in Canada, you will be asked to spell your personal information on an almost daily basis. From registering for provincial health insurance (RAMQ in Québec or OHIP in Ontario) to opening a bank account, setting up your mobile phone plan, and confirming your reservation at a clinic, administrative agents will routinely ask: *"Pouvez-vous épeler votre nom, s'il vous plaît ?"* (*Can you spell your name, please?*).

For the TCF Canada candidate, alphabet mastery has immediate score implications. In the first eight items of the *Compréhension orale* (Listening) section, examiners regularly test candidates with brief recorded telephone messages, voicemail bookings, and reception dialogues where names, email addresses, street names, or alphanumeric reference codes are spelled out letter-by-letter. If you confuse **E** with **I**, or **G** with **J**, you will select the wrong multiple-choice answer.

In this lesson, you will master the exact pronunciation of all 26 letters of the French alphabet, explore the deceptive English-French letter traps, learn how the five French accents (*l'accent aigu*, *l'accent grave*, *l'accent circonflexe*, *le tréma*, and *la cédille*) transform sounds and meanings, and practice spelling real Canadian postal codes, names, and email addresses according to authentic Francophone conventions.

### Lesson overview

The French alphabet uses the exact same 26 Latin letters as the English alphabet. However, the names of these letters—and the acoustic targets they represent—differ significantly.

### The three classic letter traps for English speakers

English speakers instinctively stumble over three specific clusters when hearing or reciting letters in French. Memorizing these three contrasts will eliminate 90% of spelling errors on the TCF Canada exam.

#### Trap 1: The G versus J Inversion
In English, the letter **G** sounds like "jee" and **J** sounds like "jay." In French, this relationship is **completely reversed**:
- **G** is called **gé** (/ʒe/) — sounds like English "jay" (with a soft /ʒ/).
- **J** is called **ji** (/ʒi/) — sounds like English "gee" (with a soft /ʒ/).

When a French speaker spells the name *Gilles* (G-I-L-L-E-S), an English speaker often writes *Jilles* because they hear `/ʒi/`. Train yourself to invert your natural reflex:
- Hearing `/ʒe/` → write **G**
- Hearing `/ʒi/` → write **J**

#### Trap 2: The E, I, and U Vowel Confusion
English vowel names mislead foreign learners:
- In English, the letter **E** is pronounced "ee." In French, "ee" is the letter **I** (/i/)!
- In French, the letter **E** is pronounced with rounded lips: **/ə/** (similar to the vowel sound in English "her" or "the").
- The French letter **U** has no equivalent in English: **/y/**. To produce it, place your tongue as if pronouncing /i/ ("ee"), but round your lips tightly as if pronouncing /u/ ("oo").

| Spoken Sound | French Letter to Write | Common English Mistake |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **/ə/** | **E** | Mistaken for "U" or "A" |
| **/i/** | **I** | Mistaken for "E" |
| **/y/** | **U** | Mistaken for "O" or "OU" |

#### Trap 3: W and Y (Double vé and I grec)
- **W**: French calls this letter **double vé** (/du.blə.ve/) because visually it resembles two connected *V*s.
- **Y**: French calls this letter **i grec** (/i.ɡʁɛk/), which literally means "Greek I" because the Romans borrowed the letter from the Greek alphabet.

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Phonetic Trap: Silent H

In the French language, the letter **H** is **never pronounced**. It produces no sound whatsoever:
- *l'homme* is pronounced `/lɔm/` (never `/hɔm/`).
- *l'hôtel* is pronounced `/lo.tɛl/`.
- *l'hôpital* is pronounced `/lo.pi.tal/`.

When spelling a word aloud, you state the letter's name: **hache** (/aʃ/). But when reading or speaking the word in a sentence, the *h* is 100% silent.

</div>

### The five French accents and their phonetic impact

French uses five diacritical marks (accents). Accents in French are **not decorative optional flourishes**; they are mandatory spelling components that radically change vowel pronunciation and grammatical meaning. Omitting an accent on the TCF Canada writing exam counts as a spelling error.

#### 1. L'accent aigu (The Acute Accent: é)
- **Appearance**: Slopes upward from left to right: **é**.
- **Rule**: Occurs **only on the letter E**.
- **Sound**: Changes the neutral /ə/ sound into a clean, closed **/e/** sound (similar to the "ay" in "say," but without gliding):
  - *le café* (/lə ka.fe/) — coffee
  - *l'été* (/le.te/) — summer
  - *répéter* (/ʁe.pe.te/) — to repeat

#### 2. L'accent grave (The Grave Accent: è, à, ù)
- **Appearance**: Slopes downward from left to right: **è**, **à**, **ù**.
- **Phonetic Impact on E**: On the letter *e* (**è**), it opens the mouth to create a wide, open **/ɛ/** sound (like the "e" in "bed" or "pet"):
  - *la mère* (/la mɛʁ/) — mother
  - *le frère* (/lə fʁɛʁ/) — brother
  - *très* (/tʁɛ/) — very
- **Grammatical Function on A and U**: On *a* and *u*, the grave accent does **not** change the pronunciation; it serves solely to distinguish homophones (words that sound identical but have different meanings):
  - **a** (has — from verb *avoir*) vs **à** (to / at — preposition)
  - **ou** (or — conjunction) vs **où** (where — interrogative/relative pronoun)
  - **la** (the — feminine article) vs **là** (there — adverb)

#### 3. L'accent circonflexe (The Circumflex: â, ê, î, ô, û)
- **Appearance**: A small hat/chevron placed over any vowel: **â, ê, î, ô, û**.
- **Historical Origin**: In Old French, these vowels were followed by the letter *s*. Over centuries, the *s* fell out of spoken pronunciation, and the circumflex was introduced to mark where the lost *s* once stood:
  - *hôpital* (formerly *hospital*)
  - *forêt* (formerly *forest*)
  - *hôtel* (formerly *hostel*)
  - *château* (formerly *chastel*)
- **Phonetic Impact**: On *ê* and *ô*, it creates an open, slightly prolonged vowel sound (*la fête* /la fɛt/, *l'hôtel* /lo.tɛl/).

#### 4. Le tréma (The Diaeresis: ë, ï, ü)
- **Appearance**: Two horizontal dots placed over the second of two adjacent vowels: **ë, ï, ü**.
- **Rule**: In French, two adjacent vowels usually combine into a single sound (e.g., *ai* = /ɛ/, *oi* = /wa/). The *tréma* instructs the reader to **pronounce the two vowels separately and distinctly**:
  - *Noël* (/nɔ.ɛl/) — Christmas (pronounced *No-el*, not *Neul*)
  - *naïf* (/na.if/) — naive (pronounced *na-if*, not *nèf*)
  - *le maïs* (/lə ma.is/) — corn / maize (pronounced *ma-iss*, not *mè*)

#### 5. La cédille (The Cedilla: ç)
- **Appearance**: A small tail attached underneath the letter **C**: **ç**.
- **Rule**: In French, the letter *c* sounds like /k/ ("hard c") before the vowels *a, o, u* (*café*, *collègue*, *cuisine*). To force a soft /s/ sound before *a, o, u*, French adds a cédille:
  - **français** (/fʁɑ̃.sɛ/) — without the cédille, it would sound like *"franka"*!
  - **le garçon** (/lə ɡaʁ.sɔ̃/) — boy / waiter
  - **ça va** (/sa va/) — it's going well
- The cédille is **never** used before *e* or *i*, because *c* is already naturally soft before those vowels (*merci*, *ceci*).

| Accent Name | French Name | Examples | Main Linguistic Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Acute** | *L'accent aigu* | **é** (*café*, *été*) | Produces closed /e/ sound on letter E |
| **Grave** | *L'accent grave* | **è, à, ù** (*mère*, *à*, *où*) | Open /ɛ/ on E; distinguishes homophones (*ou/où*) |
| **Circumflex** | *L'accent circonflexe* | **â, ê, î, ô, û** (*fête*, *hôtel*) | Marks historical lost *s*; prolongs vowel |
| **Diaeresis** | *Le tréma* | **ë, ï** (*Noël*, *naïf*, *maïs*) | Separates adjacent vowels into two syllables |
| **Cedilla** | *La cédille* | **ç** (*français*, *garçon*, *ça*) | Softens letter C to /s/ sound before *a, o, u* |

### Spelling conventions: Names, emails, and Canadian postal codes

When spelling out personal information in French, native speakers use specific formulas for punctuation marks, special characters, and uppercase/lowercase letters.

Observe how native speakers dictate contact information:

> **Comment vous appelez-vous ?**  
> *What is your name?*  
> **Je m'appelle Marc Tremblay : M-A-R-C, T-R-E-M-B-L-A-Y.**  
> /ʒə ma.pɛl maʁk tʁɑ̃.blɛ : ɛm-a-ɛʁ-se, te-ɛʁ-ə-ɛm-be-ɛl-a-i.ɡʁɛk/

#### Dictating Symbols and Punctuation:
When reciting an email address, website, or reference number, use these terms:
- **@** = **arobase** (/a.ʁɔ.baz/)
- **.** = **point** (/pwɛ̃/)
- **-** = **tiret** (/ti.ʁɛ/) or **tiret du milieu**
- **_** = **tiret bas** (/ti.ʁe ba/) or **souligné**
- **en majuscule** (/ɑ̃ ma.ʒys.kyl/) = in uppercase / capital letter
- **en minuscule** (/ɑ̃ mi.nys.kyl/) = in lowercase

Observe this email address read aloud:
> **david.tremblay@email.ca**  
> *david (point) tremblay (arobase) email (point) c-a (en minuscules)*  
> /da.vid pwɛ̃ tʁɑ̃.blɛ a.ʁɔ.baz i.mɛjl pwɛ̃ se-a/

#### Canadian Alphanumeric Postal Codes
In Canada, postal codes consist of six alphanumeric characters in the format **A1A 1A1** (alternating letter, number, letter, space, number, letter, number):
- **H2X 1Y4** (Montréal) is spelled aloud as:  
  **H-deux-X, un-Y-quatre** (/aʃ dø iks, œ̃ i.ɡʁɛk katʁ/)
- **K1P 5J2** (Ottawa) is spelled aloud as:  
  **K-un-P, cinq-J-deux** (/ka œ̃ pe, sɛ̃k ʒi dø/)

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In France, the standard keyboard layout is **AZERTY**, where the top row starts with A-Z-E-R-T-Y and numbers require pressing the Shift key. In Québec and across Canadian federal offices, the standard keyboard is **QWERTY Canadian Multilingual** (often called *le clavier canadien-français*). It retains the standard QWERTY layout for fast English typing, but provides direct, dedicated keys for **é**, **è**, **à**, and **ç**. Learning this Canadian keyboard layout will dramatically boost your typing speed for TCF Writing Task 1, 2, and 3.

</div>

In TCF Canada Listening items 1 to 6, audio prompts frequently feature a receptionist writing down a caller's name or reservation code: *"Votre nom s'écrit bien D-U-P-O-N-T ?"* Listen for final consonant traps (*T* vs *D* vs *S*) and vowel traps (*E* vs *I*). Train your ear so that hearing `/ʒi/` instantly triggers **J** and hearing `/ʒe/` instantly triggers **G**.
