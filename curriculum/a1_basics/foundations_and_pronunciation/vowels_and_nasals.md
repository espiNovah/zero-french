### Introduction

If English vowels are a moving target, French vowels are a bullseye. In English, vowels constantly glide from one sound to another (diphthongs)—when you say the word "day," your tongue moves from /e/ to /i/; when you say "go," your lips transition from /o/ to /u/. In French, gliding is strictly forbidden. French vowels are **pure monophthongs**: your lips, jaw, and tongue snap into an exact acoustic shape and hold that shape rigidly until the sound finishes.

Even more distinct are the French **nasal vowels**. In standard English, air escapes almost entirely through the mouth. In French, several high-frequency vowels redirect air simultaneously through the oral cavity and the nasal passage with the velum (soft palate) lowered. These nasal vowels are not optional stylistic ornaments; they are the grammatical backbone of the language. Confusing an oral vowel with a nasal vowel, or confusing the three nasal vowels with one another, instantly changes meaning and grammatical category: *beau* (handsome) versus *bon* (good); *bas* (low) versus *banc* (bench); *canadien* (masculine) versus *canadienne* (feminine).

In this lesson, you will master the physical mechanics of French pure vowels, conquer the infamous **U** (/y/) versus **OU** (/u/) distinction, understand the rounded **EU** vowels, master the three core French nasal vowels (**/ɑ̃/**, **/ɔ̃/**, **/ɛ̃/**), explore semi-vowel glides (*oi*, *ui*, *ille*), and discover how Canadian French preserves historical phonetic distinctions that have vanished in modern Paris.

### The mechanics of French pure vowels

To produce authentic French vowels, you must first train your articulatory muscles to stop diphthongizing. 

Compare how the mouth behaves in English versus French:
- In English "no", the lips start round and close tighter into an "oo" shape (/noʊ/).
- In French **non** (/nɔ̃/) or **mot** (/mo/), the lips lock into a fixed position and do not move.

Observe these foundational pure oral vowels:

| French Vowel Sound | IPA Symbol | Common Spellings | French Examples with Pronunciation & Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pure A** | /a/ | a, à | **ami** (/a.mi/ — *friend*), **là** (/la/ — *there*) |
| **Closed E** | /e/ | é, -er, -ez | **été** (/e.te/ — *summer*), **parler** (/paʁ.le/ — *to speak*) |
| **Open E** | /ɛ/ | è, ê, ai, ei | **mère** (/mɛʁ/ — *mother*), **faire** (/fɛʁ/ — *to do*) |
| **Pure I** | /i/ | i, y | **ici** (/i.si/ — *here*), **stylo** (/sti.lo/ — *pen*) |
| **Closed O** | /o/ | o, au, eau | **bureau** (/by.ʁo/ — *desk*), **chaud** (/ʃo/ — *hot*) |
| **Open O** | /ɔ/ | o (in closed syllable) | **porte** (/pɔʁt/ — *door*), **pomme** (/pɔm/ — *apple*) |
| **Pure OU** | /u/ | ou, où | **vous** (/vu/ — *you*), **où** (/u/ — *where*) |
| **Pure U** | /y/ | u, û | **tu** (/ty/ — *you*), **sur** (/syʁ/ — *on*) |

### The U versus OU challenge: Mastering the rounded front vowel

The single most notorious sound challenge for English speakers learning French is the contrast between **U** (/y/) and **OU** (/u/). In English, both spellings often trigger a generic "oo" sound. In French, substituting one for the other changes the meaning of fundamental words:
- **tu** (/ty/) = *you* vs **tout** (/tu/) = *all / everything*
- **vu** (/vy/) = *seen* vs **vous** (/vu/) = *you (formal/plural)*
- **sur** (/syʁ/) = *on* vs **sourd** (/suʁ/) = *deaf*
- **dessus** (/də.sy/) = *on top* vs **dessous** (/də.su/) = *underneath*

#### The Physical Recipe to Produce French /y/:
To master this sound mechanically without guessing:
1. Open your mouth and make a clean, continuous **"ee"** sound (as in "cheese" — IPA **/i/**). Notice where your tongue is: pressed firmly forward against the backs of your lower front teeth.
2. **Keep your tongue frozen in that exact "ee" position.** Do not move it backward.
3. While continuing to vocalize, **round your lips tightly forward into a tight circle**, as if you were whistling or blowing out a candle.
4. The sound that comes out is the pure French **/y/**!

Compare these paired minimal pairs:

> **Tu as vu le film ?** — /ty a vy lə film/  
> *Did you see the movie? (Pure /y/ sound)*

> **Vous écoutez tout le temps.** — /vu.z‿e.ku.te tu lə tɑ̃/  
> *You listen all the time. (Pure /u/ sound)*

> **Le livre est sur la table, pas dessous.** — /lə livʁ ɛ syʁ la tabl, pa də.su/  
> *The book is on the table, not underneath.*

### The EU and OEU vowels: Open vs closed rounded sounds

French has a distinctive family of rounded mid-vowels represented by the spellings **eu** and **œu**. They sound like a blend of "eh" and "oh", produced with rounded lips.

French distinguishes two variations of this sound:

#### 1. The Closed Sound: /ø/
- **Lip Position**: Rounded and tightly closed.
- **When it occurs**: Usually at the end of a word or before a silent consonant (*-x, -t, -s*):
  - **deux** (/dø/) — two
  - **bleu** (/blø/) — blue
  - **peu** (/pø/) — little / few
  - **je veux** (/ʒə vø/) — I want

#### 2. The Open Sound: /œ/
- **Lip Position**: Rounded but with a more open jaw.
- **When it occurs**: In syllables ending in an audible consonant (especially *r, l, f*):
  - **la sœur** (/la sœʁ/) — sister
  - **le cœur** (/lə kœʁ/) — heart
  - **l'heure** (/lœʁ/) — hour / time
  - **le professeur** (/lə pʁɔ.fɛ.sœʁ/) — teacher

Observe them in a single natural sentence:
> **Mon jeune frère a deux sœurs.** — /mɔ̃ ʒœn fʁɛʁ a dø sœʁ/  
> *My young brother has two sisters. (Notice: "jeune" /œ/ open, "deux" /ø/ closed, "sœurs" /œ/ open)*

### Nasal vowels: The three acoustic signatures

Nasal vowels are created when the air passage from the throat splits: part of the air stream exits through the mouth, while the rest resonates through the nasal cavity because the velum is lowered.

Standard international French features **three core nasal vowels**. Master their spellings and distinct mouth openings:

```
          /ɛ̃/ (Open mouth, smiling lips, tongue forward)
         /    \
        /      \
      /ɑ̃/      /ɔ̃/
 (Wide jaw)    (Round tight lips, back of throat)
```

#### 1. The Open Back Nasal: /ɑ̃/
- **Spellings**: **an**, **am**, **en**, **em** (when followed by a consonant or at the end of a word).
- **Mouth Position**: Jaw drops wide open, lips relaxed, air resonates through nasal passage (like saying "ah" through your nose):
  - **la France** (/la fʁɑ̃s/) — France
  - **l'enfant** (/lɑ̃.fɑ̃/) — child
  - **le temps** (/lə tɑ̃/) — weather / time
  - **manger** (/mɑ̃.ʒe/) — to eat

#### 2. The Rounded Closed Nasal: /ɔ̃/
- **Spellings**: **on**, **om**.
- **Mouth Position**: Lips rounded tightly into a small circle, jaw almost closed, air resonates deeply in the back (like saying "oh" through your nose):
  - **bon** (/bɔ̃/) — good (masc.)
  - **le nom** (/lə nɔ̃/) — name
  - **la maison** (/la mɛ.zɔ̃/) — house
  - **ils vont** (/il vɔ̃/) — they go

#### 3. The Spread Front Nasal: /ɛ̃/
- **Spellings**: **in**, **im**, **ain**, **aim**, **ein**, **ien**, **yn**, **ym**.
- **Mouth Position**: Lips stretched sideways into a broad smile, mouth slightly open, sound focused forward:
  - **le matin** (/lə ma.tɛ̃/) — morning
  - **le vin** (/lə vɛ̃/) — wine
  - **le pain** (/lə pɛ̃/) — bread
  - **canadien** (/ka.na.djɛ̃/) — Canadian (masc.)
  - **plein** (/plɛ̃/) — full

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Grammar Trap: The Denasalization Rule

A vowel is **only nasal** when followed by *n* or *m* that is either at the very end of a word (*bon*) or followed by another consonant (*chambre*).
When the *n* or *m* is followed by a **vowel** or another **n/m**, the nasality disappears completely, and the vowel becomes an ordinary oral vowel:

- **un ami** (/œ̃.n‿a.mi/): *ami* has a vowel after *m*, so *a* is oral /a/.
- **bon** (/bɔ̃/ — nasal) → **bonne** (/bɔn/ — oral /ɔ/ + clear /n/ sound).
- **canadien** (/ka.na.djɛ̃/ — nasal) → **canadienne** (/ka.na.djɛn/ — oral /ɛ/ + clear /n/).
- **cousin** (/ku.zɛ̃/ — nasal) → **cousine** (/ku.zin/ — oral /i/ + clear /n/).

This rule is the foundation of masculine and feminine grammatical agreement in French spoken comprehension.

</div>

### Semi-vowels and gliding sounds: OI, UI, and ILLE

In addition to pure vowels and nasals, French contains three rapid glide sounds (semi-vowels) that transition instantly into another vowel:

#### 1. The /wa/ Glide (Spelled "oi")
The spelling **oi** never sounds like "oy" in English. It is a rapid transition from /w/ to open /a/:
- **bonsoir** (/bɔ̃.swaʁ/) — good evening
- **moi** (/mwa/) — me
- **trois** (/tʁwa/) — three
- **la voiture** (/la vwa.tyʁ/) — car

#### 2. The /ɥi/ Glide (Spelled "ui")
The spelling **ui** combines the tight French /y/ sound with /i/, creating a rapid whistling glide:
- **la nuit** (/la nɥi/) — night
- **huit** (/ɥit/) — eight
- **la cuisine** (/la kɥi.zin/) — kitchen
- **suis** (/sɥi/) — am (*je suis*)

#### 3. The /j/ Glide (Spelled "-ille", "y", or "i + vowel")
Sounds like the English "y" in "yes":
- **la famille** (/la fa.mij/) — family (notice the *ll* is silent; it sounds like *fa-mee-y*)
- **travailler** (/tʁa.va.je/) — to work
- **le billet** (/lə bi.jɛ/) — ticket

### Canadian French vowel characteristics and nasal preservation

Canadian French (especially spoken in Québec, New Brunswick, and Ontario) preserves historical phonetic features of classical 17th-century French that have evolved or simplified in modern European French.

#### 1. Preservation of the Fourth Nasal Vowel: /œ̃/
In historical French, there were four distinct nasal vowels: /ɑ̃/, /ɔ̃/, /ɛ̃/, and **/œ̃/** (spelled **un**, **um**).
- In modern France, the sound **/œ̃/** has virtually merged into **/ɛ̃/**: native Parisians pronounce *un brin* (a sprig) and *un brun* (a brown one) identically as `/œ̃ bʁɛ̃/`.
- In Québec and French Canada, native speakers strictly preserve the difference:
  - **brin** (/bʁɛ̃/) — pronounced with lips smiling
  - **brun** (/bʁœ̃/) — pronounced with lips rounded forward

On the TCF Canada, both pronunciations are universally recognized, but Canadian listening audio will clearly feature the rounded **/œ̃/** in words like *un*, *lundi*, and *brun*.

#### 2. Vowel Diphthongization in Accented Syllables
In casual Québec French, long vowels with circumflex accents (*fête*, *père*, *même*) often diphthongize (e.g. *fête* sounds like /faɛ̯t/), and short high vowels in closed syllables (*midi*, *musique*, *vite*) are pronounced with lax vowels ([ɪ], [ʏ], [ʊ]). Recognizing these cadences will prevent disorientation when taking the listening test in Canada.

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

When taking the TCF Canada, you will hear a balance of international and Canadian accents:
- In European French: *un café* is pronounced `/ɛ̃ ka.fe/`.
- In Québec French **(QC)**: *un café* is pronounced `/œ̃ ka.fe/`, with tight lip rounding on *un*.
Train your ear to recognize both variations, as listening prompts in Canada feature authentic local voices.
