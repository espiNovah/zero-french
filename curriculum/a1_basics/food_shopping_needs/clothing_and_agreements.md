### Introduction

In the previous lessons, you navigated grocery stores and mastered ordering at cafés. Now, we expand your communicative toolkit to another essential everyday domain: **shopping for clothing, dressing for different climates, and mastering the grammar of French adjective agreements**.

Whether shopping for clothing at a department store in Paris or outfitting yourself for your first Canadian winter in Montreal, Ottawa, or Calgary, you must be able to describe garments, ask for specific colors and sizes, and understand store clerks. Furthermore, describing clothing is a cornerstone of **French descriptive grammar**. In French, unlike English where adjectives are static (a *blue* shirt, *blue* shoes), French adjectives behave like chameleons: they must change their spelling and pronunciation to match the **gender** (masculine or feminine) and **number** (singular or plural) of the noun they describe.

In the TCF Canada exam, adjective agreement is a primary scoring criterion under *Correction grammaticale*. On the **Speaking test (*Expression orale — Tâche 1 et 2*)**, describing people's appearance, clothing items, or shopping for products requires effortless agreement. In **TCF Listening (*Compréhension orale*)**, dialogues set in clothing stores frequently test your ability to catch subtle color, size, and price distractors. In this lesson, you will master essential wardrobe vocabulary, conquer the rules of adjective agreement, navigate invariable color traps, and learn essential Canadian winter clothing terminology.

- *Je porte un pantalon noir.* (/ʒə pɔʁt œ̃ pɑ̃.ta.lɔ̃ nwaʁ/ — *I am wearing black pants.*)
- *Elle porte une robe rouge.* (/ɛl pɔʁt yn ʁɔb ʁuʒ/ — *She is wearing a red dress.*)

#### Québec vs France: Dressing for Canadian Winters

If you are preparing to live in Canada, standard European French winter terms will be replaced by specific Québec French lexical items:

- **Une tuque** **(QC)** vs **Un bonnet** (France): In France, a knitted winter beanie is *un bonnet*. In Canada, it is universally called *une tuque* **(QC)**. Surviving the winter without a warm tuque is impossible!
- **Des mitaines** **(QC)** vs **Des moufles** (France): In Québec, mittens (gloves without individual fingers) are called *des mitaines* **(QC)**. Note: in France, *une mitaine* refers to fingerless gloves, whereas in Canada *des mitaines* are thick, warm winter mittens. Regular five-finger gloves are *des gants* (/de ɡɑ̃/) in both countries.
- **Un chandail** **(QC)** vs **Un pull** (France): While Europeans say *un pull*, Canadians typically say *un chandail* **(QC)** for any knit sweater, jersey, or sweatshirt.
- **Des espadrilles** **(QC)** vs **Des baskets** (France): In France, sneakers or athletic trainers are *des baskets*. In Québec, athletic sneakers are called *des espadrilles* **(QC)** (or familiarly, *des souliers de course*).
- **Un manteau d'hiver** **(QC)**: A heavy-duty down winter parka is commonly called *un manteau d'hiver* or *un habit de neige* (snowsuit for children).

### Adjective agreement rules: Gender and number

In English, adjectives never change: *a green jacket*, *two green jackets*, *a green car*, *two green cars*. The word "green" stays identical.

In French, **adjectives agree in gender (masculine or feminine) and number (singular or plural) with the noun they modify**:

> **Formula**: Noun [Gender + Number] $\rightarrow$ Adjective [Gender + Number]

#### 1. The Feminine Agreement (Adding -e)

The general rule for forming the feminine of an adjective is to **add an -e** to the masculine singular form:

- *bleu* (masculine) $\rightarrow$ **bleue** (feminine)
- *noir* (masculine) $\rightarrow$ **noire** (feminine)
- *court* (masculine) $\rightarrow$ **courte** (feminine)

If the masculine adjective **already ends in an unaccented -e**, it **does not change** in the feminine:
- *un pantalon rouge* $\rightarrow$ *une jupe **rouge*** (no double -e!)
- *un t-shirt jaune* $\rightarrow$ *une chemise **jaune***
- *un chapeau rose* $\rightarrow$ *une robe **rose***

#### 2. The Acoustic Power of Feminine -e: Waking Up Silent Consonants

In Section 1, you learned that French final consonants are generally silent unless followed by a vowel. When you add a feminine **-e**, it creates a new syllable and **causes the previously silent final consonant to be clearly pronounced**:

Observe how the pronunciation transforms when switching from masculine to feminine:

| Adjective | Masculine Form & IPA | Feminine Form & IPA | English Meaning | Phonetic Shift |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **vert** | **vert** (/vɛʁ/) | **verte** (/vɛʁt/) | green | Final /t/ wakes up! |
| **gris** | **gris** (/ɡʁi/) | **grise** (/ɡʁiz/) | grey | Final *s* becomes /z/ sound! |
| **blanc** | **blanc** (/blɑ̃/) | **blanche** (/blɑ̃ʃ/) | white | Nasal becomes /ɑ̃/ + /ʃ/! |
| **chaud** | **chaud** (/ʃo/) | **chaude** (/ʃod/) | warm / hot | Final /d/ wakes up! |
| **froid** | **froid** (/fʁwa/) | **froide** (/fʁwad/) | cold | Final /d/ wakes up! |
| **grand** | **grand** (/ɡʁɑ̃/) | **grande** (/ɡʁɑ̃d/) | tall / large | Final /d/ wakes up! |
| **petit** | **petit** (/pə.ti/) | **petite** (/pə.tit/) | small / short | Final /t/ wakes up! |
| **court** | **court** (/kuʁ/) | **courte** (/kuʁt/) | short (length) | Final /t/ wakes up! |

> **Un manteau vert** (/œ̃ mɑ̃.to vɛʁ/) — Final *t* is completely silent.  
> **Une écharpe verte** (/yn e.ʃaʁp vɛʁt/) — Final *t* is crisply pronounced!

> **Un pantalon gris** (/œ̃ pɑ̃.ta.lɔ̃ ɡʁi/) — Final *s* is completely silent.  
> **Une veste grise** (/yn vɛst ɡʁiz/) — Final *s* is pronounced as a voiced /z/!

#### 3. The Plural Agreement (Adding -s)

To make an adjective plural, the general rule is to **add an -s** to the singular form:
- Masculine plural: add **-s** to masculine singular (*vert* $\rightarrow$ **verts** /vɛʁ/)
- Feminine plural: add **-s** to feminine singular (*verte* $\rightarrow$ **vertes** /vɛʁt/)

Just like nouns, the plural **-s** on an adjective is **completely silent** in pronunciation:
- *Il porte des pantalons verts.* (/il pɔʁt de pɑ̃.ta.lɔ̃ vɛʁ/) — Sounds identical to singular *vert*!
- *Elle porte des chaussures vertes.* (/ɛl pɔʁt de ʃo.syʁ vɛʁt/) — Sounds identical to singular *verte*!

Observe the complete 4-part agreement matrix for the adjective **noir** (black):

| | Masculine Singular | Feminine Singular | Masculine Plural | Feminine Plural |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Spelling** | **noir** | **noire** | **noirs** | **noires** |
| **Pronunciation** | /nwaʁ/ | /nwaʁ/ | /nwaʁ/ | /nwaʁ/ |
| **Example** | *un manteau noir* | *une veste noire* | *des manteaux noirs* | *des vestes noires* |

#### Critical Grammar Trap: Invariable Colors (Marron and Orange)

Some colors in French originate from physical objects (fruits, nuts, or stones). When a color word is derived from a noun, it is **invariable**: it **never** takes an feminine *-e* and **never** takes a plural *-s*!

The two most common invariable colors on the TCF exam are:
1. **marron** (/ma.ʁɔ̃/, chestnut / brown):
   - *un pantalon marron*
   - *une veste marron* (never *"marrone"*)
   - *des chaussures marron* (never *"marrons"*)
2. **orange** (/ɔ.ʁɑ̃ʒ/, orange):
   - *un chapeau orange*
   - *une tuque orange*
   - *des t-shirts orange* (never *"oranges"*)

Writing *"des chaussures marronnes"* or *"des robes oranges"* is a severe grammatical error on TCF Writing Task 1.

</div>

### Adjective placement and shopping mechanics

Where do French adjectives go? In English, adjectives always precede the noun: *a red dress*, *a comfortable sweater*.

In French, the general rule is the reverse: **most adjectives go AFTER the noun**:

> **Rule**: Noun + Adjective  
> **une chemise blanche** (a white shirt)  
> **un manteau chaud** (a warm coat)  
> **un pull confortable** (a comfortable sweater)  
> **des chaussures élégantes** (elegant shoes)

All color adjectives, shape adjectives, and nationality adjectives **always follow the noun**:
- *des bottes italiennes* (Italian boots)
- *une table ronde* (a round table)
- *un pantalon bleu* (blue pants)

#### The BANGS Exceptions: Adjectives That Precede the Noun

A small group of frequent, short adjectives are placed **before the noun**. You can easily memorize them with the acronym **BANGS**:

- **B**eauty: **beau / belle** (*un beau manteau*, *une belle robe*)
- **A**ge: **jeune, vieux / vieille, nouveau / nouvelle** (*un vieux pull*, *une nouvelle veste*)
- **N**umber: **premier, deuxième, deux, trois** (*la première fois*, *deux chemises*)
- **G**oodness: **bon / bonne, mauvais / mauvaise** (*un bon manteau*, *une mauvaise idée*)
- **S**ize: **grand / grande, petit / petite, gros / grosse** (*un grand magasin*, *une petite taille*)

> **Un beau manteau noir** (/œ̃ bo mɑ̃.to nwaʁ/)  
> Notice the placement: *beau* (Beauty) comes **before** the noun, while *noir* (Color) comes **after**!

#### Essential Shopping Phrases in a Clothing Store

When shopping for clothes or taking a TCF speaking test, you will need these practical expressions:

| French Expression | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning | Usage Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Quelle est votre taille ?** | /kɛ.l‿ɛ vɔtʁ taj/ | What is your (clothing) size? | Asked by the sales clerk for shirts, pants, and dresses. |
| **Je fais du 38 / du M.** | /ʒə fɛ dy tʁɑ̃t.ɥit/ | I wear size 38 / medium. | Note the formula: *faire du + size*. |
| **Quelle est votre pointure ?** | /kɛ.l‿ɛ vɔtʁ pwɛ̃.tyʁ/ | What is your shoe size? | *La pointure* is strictly reserved for shoes! |
| **Je fais du 42.** | /ʒə fɛ dy ka.ʁɑ̃t.dø/ | I wear shoe size 42. | European shoe sizes (42 EU $\approx$ 9 US/Canada). |
| **Je peux l'essayer ?** | /ʒə pø l‿e.sɛ.je/ | May I try it on? | Used with the verb *essayer* (to try on). |
| **Où sont les cabines d'essayage ?** | /u sɔ̃ le ka.bin d‿e.sɛ.jaʒ/ | Where are the fitting rooms? | *La cabine d'essayage* = the changing/fitting room. |
| **C'est trop grand / trop petit.** | /s‿ɛ tʁo ɡʁɑ̃/ / /tʁo pə.ti/ | It's too big / too small. | Expressing sizing feedback. |
| **Ça vous va très bien !** | /sa vu va tʁɛ bjɛ̃/ | It suits you very well! | The standard compliment from a French clerk. |

#### Exam Tip: Distractor Traps with Colors and Sizes in TCF Listening

On the TCF Listening exam, retail dialogues between customers and store associates contain predictable distractor patterns:

1. **The Out-of-Stock Pivot**:  
   - Customer: *"Bonjour, je cherche cette robe bleue en taille 38."* (I'm looking for this blue dress in size 38.)  
   - Clerk: *"Ah, désolé madame, nous n'avons plus de taille 38 en bleu. En revanche, il me reste du 38 en vert ou du 40 en bleu."*  
   - Question: *Quelle robe est disponible en taille 38 ?*  
   - **Trap**: The answer options will include *la robe bleue en 38* (distractor), *la robe bleue en 40*, and *la robe verte en 38* (correct answer!).
2. **Taille vs Pointure**:  
   If an audio prompt discusses footwear, the question may test whether you recognize *la pointure* versus *la taille*. Never confuse the two: *la taille* is for clothing, *la pointure* is strictly for shoes.
