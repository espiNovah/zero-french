### Introduction

In previous lessons, you learned how to introduce yourself, discuss your family, describe your living quarters, and conjugate regular and irregular verbs to talk about your daily schedule. Now, it is time to tackle one of the most vital survival skills for living in a Francophone country and succeeding on the TCF Canada: **buying food, navigating grocery stores, and expressing quantities**.

When English speakers talk about food, they say "I eat bread" or "I drink water" without inserting an article. In French, however, a noun can almost never stand alone without a determiner. You cannot simply say *"Je mange pain"*; French grammar requires you to specify whether you are eating an entire loaf, bread in general, or an unspecified portion of bread. This is where **partitive articles** (*les articles partitifs* : **du**, **de la**, **de l'**, **des**) come in.

In the TCF Canada examination, food and grocery transactions are common topics in **Listening (*Compréhension orale*)**—such as announcements in supermarkets, dialogues at the bakery counter, and voice messages leaving shopping lists—as well as in **Speaking Task 2 (*Expression orale — Exercice en interaction*)**, where you must ask an examiner detailed questions about prices, ingredients, and store hours. In this lesson, you will master the communicative logic of partitives, contrast them with definite and indefinite articles, navigate the famous "negative *de*" trap, build an essential culinary and grocery vocabulary, and discover key Canadian grocery conventions.

### Countable vs uncountable: The logic of partitive articles

In English, you can distinguish between things you count as discrete individual units (one apple, two carrots, three eggs) and things you perceive as a mass, liquid, or continuous substance (bread, milk, rice, cheese, water). You do not normally say "I want three milks" unless you mean three bottles or glasses; you say "I want some milk" or simply "I want milk."

In French, every common noun requires an article. To express an **unspecified quantity of an uncountable substance**—the equivalent of English "some" or "any", or an unspecified amount where English uses no word at all—French uses the **partitive article** (*l'article partitif*).

The partitive article indicates that you are taking, consuming, or discussing an **unmeasured portion of a whole**:

> **Je bois de l'eau.** — /ʒə bwa də l‿o/ (zhuh bwah duh LOH) — *I drink (some) water.*  
> **Je mange du pain.** — /ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy pɛ̃/ (zhuh mahnzh dew PEHN) — *I eat (some) bread.*  
> **Elle achète de la viande.** — /ɛ.l‿a.ʃɛt də la vjɑ̃d/ (el-ah-SHET duh lah VYAHND) — *She buys (some) meat.*  
> **Nous prenons des pâtes.** — /nu pʁə.nɔ̃ de pat/ (noo pruh-NOHN day PAHT) — *We have (some) pasta.*

The form of the partitive article depends entirely on the **gender** and **number** of the noun, as well as whether the noun begins with a vowel or silent *h*:

| Noun Category | Partitive Article | IPA | Respelling | Formula | Example in Context | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine Singular (Consonant)** | **du** | /dy/ | (DEW) | *de + le* | **du fromage** (/dy fʁɔ.maʒ/ — (dew froh-MAHZH)) | (some) cheese |
| **Feminine Singular (Consonant)** | **de la** | /də la/ | (duh LAH) | *de + la* | **de la soupe** (/də la sup/ — (duh lah SOOP)) | (some) soup |
| **Singular (Vowel or Silent H)** | **de l'** | /də l/ | (duh L) | *de + l'* | **de l'ail** (/də l‿aj/ — (duh LEI)), **de l'eau** (/də l‿o/ — (duh LOH)) | (some) garlic, (some) water |
| **Plural (Masculine or Feminine)** | **des** | /de/ | (DAY) | *de + les* | **des épinards** (/de.z‿e.pi.naʁ/ — (day-zay-pee-NAR)) | (some) spinach |

Just like the preposition contractions you learned in municipal navigation (*de + le = du*, *de + les = des*), the partitive forms **du** and **des** are contracted forms. You can never say *"de le pain"* or *"de les pâtes"*.

### Three way contrast: Partitive vs indefinite vs definite

One of the most frequent errors made by language learners on both the TCF Writing and Speaking tests is selecting the wrong article before food nouns. In French, your choice of article changes the meaning completely.

Observe this comparison with the masculine noun **fromage** (/fʁɔ.maʒ/ — (froh-MAHZH), cheese):

1. **Partitive (*du*) = An unmeasured portion or slice**:  
   > **Je mange du fromage.** — /ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy fʁɔ.maʒ/ (zhuh mahnzh dew froh-MAHZH)  
   > *I am eating some cheese / I eat cheese. (A portion from a larger block)*

2. **Indefinite (*un / une*) = A whole, countable unit**:  
   > **J'achète un fromage.** — /ʒ‿a.ʃɛt œ̃ fʁɔ.maʒ/ (zhah-SHET uhn froh-MAHZH)  
   > *I am buying a cheese. (An entire round, wheel, or package)*

3. **Definite (*le / la / l' / les*) = A specific item OR a general category**:  
   > **Je passe le fromage.** — /ʒə pɑs lə fʁɔ.maʒ/ (zhuh pahss luh froh-MAHZH)  
   > *I pass the cheese. (That specific cheese on the table)*  
   > **J'aime le fromage.** — /ʒ‿ɛm lə fʁɔ.maʒ/ (zhem luh froh-MAHZH)  
   > *I like cheese. (Cheese as a general concept)*

#### Verbs of Preference Always Take Definite Articles

Whenever you use verbs expressing taste, affection, preference, or hatred—such as **aimer** (/e.me/ — (ay-MAY), to like/love), **adorer** (/a.dɔ.ʁe/ — (ah-doh-RAY), to adore), **préférer** (/pʁe.fe.ʁe/ — (pray-fay-RAY), to prefer), and **détester** (/de.tɛs.te/ — (day-tess-TAY), to hate)—you are making a judgment about the **entire category** of that food, never just a portion. Therefore, verbs of preference are **always followed by definite articles** (*le, la, l', les*), never partitives:

> **J'adore le café, mais le soir je bois du thé.**  
> /ʒ‿a.dɔʁ lə ka.fe | mɛ lə swaʁ ʒə bwa dy te/  
> (zhah-DOR luh kah-FAY, meh luh swahr zhuh bwah dew TAY)  
> *I love coffee (general category), but in the evening I drink (some) tea (unmeasured portion).*

> **Elle déteste le poisson, alors elle commande de la viande.**  
> /ɛl de.tɛst lə pwa.sɔ̃ | a.lɔʁ ɛl kɔ.mɑ̃d də la vjɑ̃d/  
> (el day-TEST luh pwah-SOHN, ah-lor el koh-MAHND duh lah VYAHND)  
> *She hates fish (general category), so she orders (some) meat (portion).*

#### Critical Grammar Trap: Verbs of Preference Never Take Du or De La

Never say *"J'aime du café"* or *"Je déteste de la salade"*. Even though in English you say "I like coffee" (with no article), French requires the definite article:
- **J'aime le café.** (/ʒ‿ɛm lə ka.fe/ — (zhem luh kah-FAY))
- **Je préfère la bière.** (/ʒə pʁe.fɛʁ la bjɛʁ/ — (zhuh pray-FEHR lah BYEHR))
- **Nous adorons les fruits.** (/nu.z‿a.dɔ.ʁɔ̃ le fʁɥi/ — (noo-zah-doh-ROHN lay FRWEE))

Using a partitive article with *aimer*, *adorer*, *préférer*, or *détester* is a basic grammar error that examiners notice immediately in TCF Writing and Speaking.

</div>

### The universal negative rule: The transformation to de

When a sentence containing a partitive article (**du**, **de la**, **de l'**, **des**) or an indefinite article (**un**, **une**, **des**) is turned into a **negative sentence** with **ne... pas**, the article vanishes and is replaced by **de** (or **d'** before a vowel sound):

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Je mange du pain.** (/ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy pɛ̃/ — (zhuh mahnzh dew PEHN)) — *I eat bread.*  
> **Negative**: **Je ne mange pas de pain.** (/ʒə nə mɑ̃ʒ pa də pɛ̃/ — (zhuh nuh mahnzh pah duh PEHN)) — *I do not eat (any) bread.*

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Il boit de la bière.** (/il bwa də la bjɛʁ/ — (eel bwah duh lah BYEHR)) — *He drinks beer.*  
> **Negative**: **Il ne boit pas de bière.** (/il nə bwa pa də bjɛʁ/ — (eel nuh bwah pah duh BYEHR)) — *He does not drink (any) beer.*

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Nous avons de l'huile.** (/nu.z‿a.vɔ̃ də l‿ɥil/ — (noo-zah-VOHN duh LWEEL)) — *We have oil.*  
> **Negative**: **Nous n'avons pas d'huile.** (/nu n‿a.vɔ̃ pa d‿ɥil/ — (noo nah-vohn pah DWEEL)) — *We do not have (any) oil.*

> **Affirmative (Indefinite)**: **J'ai une pomme.** (/ʒ‿e yn pɔm/ — (zhay ewn POM)) — *I have an apple.*  
> **Negative**: **Je n'ai pas de pomme.** (/ʒə n‿e pa də pɔm/ — (zhuh nay pah duh POM)) — *I do not have an apple / any apples.*

The communicative logic is intuitive: when there is zero quantity (*ne... pas*), you are expressing a complete absence, so the partitive ("some") cannot exist.

#### The Single Exception: The Verb Être

The only common exception to this rule is the verb **être** (to be). When negating *être*, the article **does not change to de**:

> **Ce n'est pas du beurre, c'est de la margarine.**  
> /sə n‿ɛ pa dy bœʁ | s‿ɛ də la maʁ.ɡa.ʁin/  
> (suh neh pah dew BEUR, seh duh lah mar-gah-REEN)  
> *This is not butter, it is margarine.*

> **Ce ne sont pas des légumes frais.**  
> /sə nə sɔ̃ pa de le.ɡym fʁɛ/  
> (suh nuhsohn pah day lay-GEWM FREH)  
> *These are not fresh vegetables.*

### Fixed quantities, containers, and measurements

When you specify an exact quantity, weight, container, or volume, the substance is no longer "unmeasured." In French, **all expressions of quantity are followed by de (or d') without any article**:

| Expression of Quantity | IPA | Respelling | English Meaning | Example in Context | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **un kilo de** | /œ̃ ki.lo də/ | (uhn kee-LOH duh) | a kilogram of | **un kilo de pommes** | a kilo of apples |
| **un demi-kilo de** | /œ̃ də.mi ki.lo də/ | (uhn duh-mee-kee-LOH duh) | half a kilo of (500g) | **un demi-kilo de farine** | 500g of flour |
| **un litre de** | /œ̃ litʁ də/ | (uhn LEE-truh duh) | a liter of | **un litre de lait** | a liter of milk |
| **une bouteille de** | /yn bu.tɛj də/ | (ewn boo-TEY duh) | a bottle of | **une bouteille de vin** | a bottle of wine |
| **un paquet de** | /œ̃ pa.kɛ də/ | (uhn pah-KEH duh) | a packet / package of | **un paquet de biscuits** | a pack of cookies |
| **une boîte de** | /yn bwat də/ | (ewn BWAHT duh) | a box / tin / can of | **une boîte de tomates** | a can of tomatoes |
| **un morceau de** | /œ̃ mɔʁ.so də/ | (uhn mor-SOH duh) | a piece of | **un morceau de fromage** | a piece of cheese |
| **une tranche de** | /yn tʁɑ̃ʃ də/ | (ewn TRAHNSH duh) | a slice of | **trois tranches de jambon** | three slices of ham |
| **beaucoup de** | /bo.ku də/ | (boh-KOO duh) | a lot of / many | **beaucoup de légumes** | lots of vegetables |
| **un peu de** | /œ̃ pø də/ | (uhn PEUH duh) | a little bit of | **un peu de sel** | a little bit of salt |
| **assez de** | /a.se də/ | (ah-SAY duh) | enough | **assez de sucre** | enough sugar |
| **trop de** | /tʁo də/ | (TROH duh) | too much / too many | **trop de gras** | too much fat |

Notice that regardless of whether the noun is singular or plural, the preposition remains simple **de** or **d'**:
- *un kilo de carottes* (never *"un kilo des carottes"*)
- *beaucoup d'eau* (never *"beaucoup de l'eau"*)
- *une bouteille d'huile* (never *"une bouteille de l'huile"*)

#### Québec vs France: Grocery Shopping Realities

If you live in or prepare to immigrate to Canada, you will encounter distinctive Canadian French vocabulary for food and shopping:

- **Faire l'épicerie** **(QC)** (/fɛʁ l‿e.pis.ʁi/ — (fehr lay-pees-REE)) vs **Faire les courses** (France) (/fɛʁ le kuʁs/ — (fehr lay KOORS)): In France, you go *faire les courses*. In Québec, everyone says *faire l'épicerie* to mean grocery shopping, while *magasiner* **(QC)** (/ma.ɡa.zi.ne/ — (mah-gah-zee-NAY)) refers to general retail shopping.
- **Le dépanneur** **(QC)** (/lə de.pa.nœʁ/ — (luh day-pah-NEUR)): In Québec, the neighbourhood corner convenience store (selling milk, bread, beer, snacks, and lottery tickets) is universally called *le dépanneur* (or familiarly, *le dep*). In France, people refer to *l'épicerie de quartier* or *la supérette*.
- **Le panier d'épicerie** **(QC)** (/œ̃ pa.nje d‿e.pis.ʁi/ — (uhn pah-nyay day-pees-REE)): While *le chariot* (or *caddie*) is standard in France for a shopping cart, in Québec people usually say *un panier d'épicerie* **(QC)** (familiarly, *un carrosse*). Don't confuse it with *un cabaret* **(QC)**, which in Québec means a serving tray.
- **Canne de conserve** **(QC)** (/yn kan də kɔ̃.sɛʁv/ — (ewn kahn duh kohn-SEHRV)): In familiar Québec French, a canned good is often called *une canne de tomates* or *une canne de soupe* **(QC)**, adapted from English "can". The OQLF advises against this anglicism; the standard term, in Québec as in France, is *une boîte de conserve* (*une boîte de tomates*).
- **Liqueur** **(QC)** (/də la li.kœʁ/ — (duh lah lee-KEUR)): In familiar Québec French, *de la liqueur* **(QC)** refers to non-alcoholic soft drinks/soda (Coke, Sprite). The OQLF advises against this use; the standard term is *une boisson gazeuse* (or *un soda*). In standard French, *une liqueur* is an alcoholic cordial.
- **Le breuvage** **(QC)** (/œ̃ bʁœ.vaʒ/ — (uhn breuh-VAHZH)): In familiar Québec French, *un breuvage* **(QC)** is often used for any beverage or drink. The OQLF advises against this use (influenced by English "beverage"); the standard word, in Québec as in France, is *une boisson*.

#### Exam Tip: TCF Listening Traps in Food & Market Transactions

On the TCF Canada Listening test, short everyday announcements and dialogues may take place at a market stall or supermarket checkout. Keep these three distractor traps in mind:

1. **The Price Distractor**: A customer might ask, *"C'est combien le kilo de cerises ?"* (How much is a kilo of cherries?). The seller answers: *"Elles sont à 8 dollars le kilo, mais aujourd'hui je fais une promotion à 5 dollars."* The multiple-choice options will list both 8 dollars and 5 dollars. You must listen for the operative reduction connector (*mais aujourd'hui, promotion*).
2. **The Missing Ingredient Trap**: In telephone messages, a speaker often says: *"N'achète pas de tomates, il m'en reste deux dans le frigo. Prends plutôt du basilic et un kilo de riz."* If a question asks what to buy, *les tomates* will be a distractor answer!
3. **Weight Units in Canada**: Canadian speakers may use both metric (*kilos*, *grammes*) and imperial units commonly heard in North American markets, such as *une livre* (one pound, approximately 454g) and *une douzaine d'œufs* (a dozen eggs).
