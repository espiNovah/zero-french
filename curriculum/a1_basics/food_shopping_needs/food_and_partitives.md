### Introduction

In previous lessons, you learned how to introduce yourself, discuss your family, describe your living quarters, and conjugate regular and irregular verbs to talk about your daily schedule. Now, it is time to tackle one of the most vital survival skills for living in a Francophone country and succeeding on the TCF Canada: **buying food, navigating grocery stores, and expressing quantities**.

When English speakers talk about food, they say "I eat bread" or "I drink water" without inserting an article. In French, however, a noun can almost never stand alone without a determiner. You cannot simply say *"Je mange pain"*; French grammar requires you to specify whether you are eating an entire loaf, bread in general, or an unspecified portion of bread. This is where **partitive articles** (*les articles partitifs* : **du**, **de la**, **de l'**, **des**) come in.

In the TCF Canada examination, food and grocery transactions appear constantly in **Listening (*Compréhension orale*)**—such as announcements in supermarkets, dialogues at the bakery counter, and voice messages leaving shopping lists—as well as in **Speaking Task 2 (*Expression orale — Exercice en interaction*)**, where you must ask an examiner detailed questions about prices, ingredients, and store hours. In this lesson, you will master the communicative logic of partitives, contrast them with definite and indefinite articles, navigate the famous "negative *de*" trap, build an essential culinary and grocery vocabulary, and discover key Canadian grocery conventions.

### Countable vs uncountable: The logic of partitive articles

In English, you can distinguish between things you count as discrete individual units (one apple, two carrots, three eggs) and things you perceive as a mass, liquid, or continuous substance (bread, milk, rice, cheese, water). You do not normally say "I want three milks" unless you mean three bottles or glasses; you say "I want some milk" or simply "I want milk."

In French, every common noun requires an article. To express an **unspecified quantity of an uncountable substance**—the equivalent of English "some" or "any", or an unspecified amount where English uses no word at all—French uses the **partitive article** (*l'article partitif*).

The partitive article indicates that you are taking, consuming, or discussing an **unmeasured portion of a whole**:

> **Je bois de l'eau.** — /ʒə bwa də l‿o/ — *I drink (some) water.*  
> **Je mange du pain.** — /ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy pɛ̃/ — *I eat (some) bread.*  
> **Elle achète de la viande.** — /ɛ.l‿a.ʃɛt də la vjɑ̃d/ — *She buys (some) meat.*  
> **Nous prenons des pâtes.** — /nu pʁə.nɔ̃ de pat/ — *We have (some) pasta.*

The form of the partitive article depends entirely on the **gender** and **number** of the noun, as well as whether the noun begins with a vowel or silent *h*:

| Noun Category | Partitive Article | IPA | Formula | Example in Context | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine Singular (Consonant)** | **du** | /dy/ | *de + le* | **du fromage** (/dy fʁɔ.maʒ/) | (some) cheese |
| **Feminine Singular (Consonant)** | **de la** | /də la/ | *de + la* | **de la soupe** (/də la sup/) | (some) soup |
| **Singular (Vowel or Silent H)** | **de l'** | /də l/ | *de + l'* | **de l'ail** (/də l‿aj/), **de l'eau** (/də l‿o/) | (some) garlic, (some) water |
| **Plural (Masculine or Feminine)** | **des** | /de/ | *de + les* | **des épinards** (/de.z‿e.pi.naʁ/) | (some) spinach |

Just like the preposition contractions you learned in municipal navigation (*de + le = du*, *de + les = des*), the partitive forms **du** and **des** are contracted forms. You can never say *"de le pain"* or *"de les pâtes"*.

### Three way contrast: Partitive vs indefinite vs definite

One of the most frequent errors made by language learners on both the TCF Writing and Speaking tests is selecting the wrong article before food nouns. In French, your choice of article changes the meaning completely.

Observe this comparison with the masculine noun **fromage** (/fʁɔ.maʒ/, cheese):

1. **Partitive (*du*) = An unmeasured portion or slice**:  
   > **Je mange du fromage.** — /ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy fʁɔ.maʒ/  
   > *I am eating some cheese / I eat cheese. (A portion from a larger block)*

2. **Indefinite (*un / une*) = A whole, countable unit**:  
   > **J'achète un fromage.** — /ʒ‿a.ʃɛt œ̃ fʁɔ.maʒ/  
   > *I am buying a cheese. (An entire round, wheel, or package)*

3. **Definite (*le / la / l' / les*) = A specific item OR a general category**:  
   > **Je passe le fromage.** — /ʒə pɑs lə fʁɔ.maʒ/  
   > *I pass the cheese. (That specific cheese on the table)*  
   > **J'aime le fromage.** — /ʒ‿ɛm lə fʁɔ.maʒ/  
   > *I like cheese. (Cheese as a general concept)*

#### Verbs of Preference Always Take Definite Articles

Whenever you use verbs expressing taste, affection, preference, or hatred—such as **aimer** (/e.me/, to like/love), **adorer** (/a.dɔ.ʁe/, to adore), **préférer** (/pʁe.fe.ʁe/, to prefer), and **détester** (/de.tɛs.te/, to hate)—you are making a judgment about the **entire category** of that food, never just a portion. Therefore, verbs of preference are **always followed by definite articles** (*le, la, l', les*), never partitives:

> **J'adore le café, mais le soir je bois du thé.**  
> /ʒ‿a.dɔʁ lə ka.fe | mɛ lə swaʁ ʒə bwa dy te/  
> *I love coffee (general category), but in the evening I drink (some) tea (unmeasured portion).*

> **Elle déteste le poisson, alors elle commande de la viande.**  
> /ɛl de.tɛst lə pwa.sɔ̃ | a.lɔʁ.z‿ɛl kɔ.mɑ̃d də la vjɑ̃d/  
> *She hates fish (general category), so she orders (some) meat (portion).*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Grammar Trap: Verbs of Preference Never Take Du or De La

Never say *"J'aime du café"* or *"Je déteste de la salade"*. Even though in English you say "I like coffee" (with no article), French requires the definite article:
- **J'aime le café.** (Correct)
- **Je préfère la bière.** (Correct)
- **Nous adorons les fruits.** (Correct)

Using a partitive article with *aimer*, *adorer*, *préférer*, or *détester* is an automatic grammar flag on the TCF assessment rubric.

### The universal negative rule: The transformation to de

When a sentence containing a partitive article (**du**, **de la**, **de l'**, **des**) or an indefinite article (**un**, **une**, **des**) is turned into a **negative sentence** with **ne... pas**, the article vanishes and is replaced by **de** (or **d'** before a vowel sound):

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Je mange du pain.** (/ʒə mɑ̃ʒ dy pɛ̃/) — *I eat bread.*  
> **Negative**: **Je ne mange pas de pain.** (/ʒə nə mɑ̃ʒ pa də pɛ̃/) — *I do not eat (any) bread.*

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Il boit de la bière.** (/il bwa də la bjɛʁ/) — *He drinks beer.*  
> **Negative**: **Il ne boit pas de bière.** (/il nə bwa pa də bjɛʁ/) — *He does not drink (any) beer.*

> **Affirmative (Partitive)**: **Nous avons de l'huile.** (/nu.z‿a.vɔ̃ də l‿ɥil/) — *We have oil.*  
> **Negative**: **Nous n'avons pas d'huile.** (/nu n‿a.vɔ̃ pa d‿ɥil/) — *We do not have (any) oil.*

> **Affirmative (Indefinite)**: **J'ai une pomme.** (/ʒ‿e yn pɔm/) — *I have an apple.*  
> **Negative**: **Je n'ai pas de pomme.** (/ʒə n‿e pa də pɔm/) — *I do not have an apple / any apples.*

The communicative logic is intuitive: when there is zero quantity (*ne... pas*), you are expressing a complete absence, so the partitive ("some") cannot exist.

#### The Single Exception: The Verb Être

The only common exception to this rule is the verb **être** (to be). When negating *être*, the article **does not change to de**:

> **Ce n'est pas du beurre, c'est de la margarine.**  
> /sə n‿ɛ pa dy bœʁ | s‿ɛ də la maʁ.ɡa.ʁin/  
> *This is not butter, it is margarine.*

> **Ce ne sont pas des légumes frais.**  
> /sə nə sɔ̃ pa de le.ɡym fʁɛ/  
> *These are not fresh vegetables.*

### Fixed quantities, containers, and measurements

When you specify an exact quantity, weight, container, or volume, the substance is no longer "unmeasured." In French, **all expressions of quantity are followed by de (or d') without any article**:

| Expression of Quantity | IPA | English Meaning | Example in Context | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **un kilo de** | /œ̃ ki.lo də/ | a kilogram of | **un kilo de pommes** | a kilo of apples |
| **un demi-kilo de** | /œ̃ də.mi ki.lo də/ | half a kilo of (500g) | **un demi-kilo de farine** | 500g of flour |
| **un litre de** | /œ̃ litʁ də/ | a liter of | **un litre de lait** | a liter of milk |
| **une bouteille de** | /yn bu.tɛj də/ | a bottle of | **une bouteille de vin** | a bottle of wine |
| **un paquet de** | /œ̃ pa.kɛ də/ | a packet / package of | **un paquet de biscuits** | a pack of cookies |
| **une boîte de** | /yn bwat də/ | a box / tin / can of | **une boîte de tomates** | a can of tomatoes |
| **un morceau de** | /œ̃ mɔʁ.so də/ | a piece of | **un morceau de fromage** | a piece of cheese |
| **une tranche de** | /yn tʁɑ̃ʃ də/ | a slice of | **trois tranches de jambon** | three slices of ham |
| **beaucoup de** | /bo.ku də/ | a lot of / many | **beaucoup de légumes** | lots of vegetables |
| **un peu de** | /œ̃ pø də/ | a little bit of | **un peu de sel** | a little bit of salt |
| **assez de** | /a.se də/ | enough | **assez de sucre** | enough sugar |
| **trop de** | /tʁo də/ | too much / too many | **trop de gras** | too much fat |

Notice that regardless of whether the noun is singular or plural, the preposition remains simple **de** or **d'**:
- *un kilo de carottes* (never *"un kilo des carottes"*)
- *beaucoup d'eau* (never *"beaucoup de l'eau"*)
- *une bouteille d'huile* (never *"une bouteille de l'huile"*)

#### Québec vs France: Grocery Shopping Realities

If you live in or prepare to immigrate to Canada, you will encounter distinctive Canadian French vocabulary for food and shopping:

- **Faire l'épicerie** **(QC)** vs **Faire les courses** (France): In France, you go *faire les courses*. In Québec, everyone says *faire l'épicerie* to mean grocery shopping, while *magasiner* **(QC)** refers to general retail shopping.
- **Le dépanneur** **(QC)**: In Québec, the neighbourhood corner convenience store (selling milk, bread, beer, snacks, and lottery tickets) is universally called *le dépanneur* (or familiarly, *le dep*). In France, people refer to *l'épicerie de quartier* or *l'arabe du coin*.
- **Le panier et le cabaret** **(QC)**: While *le chariot* (or *caddie*) is standard in France for a shopping cart, in Québec people often ask for *un panier d'épicerie* or *un cabaret* **(QC)**.
- **Canne de conserve** **(QC)**: In Québec, a canned good is frequently called *une canne de tomates* or *une canne de soupe* **(QC)**, adapted from English "can", whereas in France it is *une boîte de conserve*.
- **Liqueur** **(QC)**: In Québec French, *de la liqueur* **(QC)** refers to non-alcoholic soft drinks/soda (Coke, Sprite), whereas in France *une liqueur* is strictly an alcoholic cordial and soda is *une boisson gazeuse* or *un soda*.
- **Le breuvage** **(QC)**: In Canadian French, *un breuvage* **(QC)** is the standard word for any beverage or drink (formal and everyday), whereas in European French the word is *une boisson*.

#### Exam Tip: TCF Listening Traps in Food & Market Transactions

On the TCF Canada Listening test, short everyday announcements and dialogues frequently take place at a market stall or supermarket checkout. Keep these three distractor traps in mind:

1. **The Price Distractor**: A customer might ask, *"C'est combien la livre de cerises ?"* (How much is a pound of cherries?). The seller answers: *"Elles sont à 8 dollars le kilo, mais aujourd'hui je fais une promotion à 5 dollars."* The multiple-choice options will list both 8 dollars and 5 dollars. You must listen for the operative reduction connector (*mais aujourd'hui, promotion*).
2. **The Missing Ingredient Trap**: In telephone messages, a speaker often says: *"N'achète pas de tomates, il m'en reste deux dans le frigo. Prends plutôt du basilic et un kilo de riz."* If a question asks what to buy, *les tomates* will be a distractor answer!
3. **Weight Units in Canada**: Canadian listening items regularly use both metric (*kilos*, *grammes*) and imperial units commonly heard in North American markets, such as *une livre* (one pound, approximately 454g) and *une douzaine d'œufs* (a dozen eggs).
