### Introduction

In the previous lesson, you explored the supermarket aisles and mastered the mechanics of partitive articles (*du, de la, de l', des*). Now, we step out of the grocery store and into the vibrant world of Francophone culinary culture: **cafés, bakeries (*boulangeries*), bistros, and counter-service restaurants**.

Whether stopping for a morning espresso and croissant in Paris, ordering a café allongé and breakfast sandwich at a Montreal *brûlerie*, or having a quick lunch near your workplace, café transactions are the ultimate test of everyday spoken French. Furthermore, in the **TCF Canada Speaking test (*Expression orale — Tâche 2: Exercice en interaction*)**, ordering food, asking for recommendations, inquiring about prices, and negotiating payment is one of the most frequently tested real-world roleplay scenarios.

In this lesson, you will learn the essential food and beverage terms for counter ordering, master the polite conditional formula **je voudrais** (and why using *je veux* will offend French speakers), learn three distinct ways to ask for prices and bills, understand payment options and tipping etiquette in France versus Canada, and discover key Canadian coffeehouse terminology.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Essential vocabulary for ordering hot beverages, cold drinks, pastries, and quick meals.
- The social mechanics of French politeness and why **je voudrais** is mandatory instead of *je veux*.
- Practical ordering formulas: *Pour moi, ce sera...*, *Est-ce que vous avez... ?*, and *Avez-vous... ?*.
- Three ways to ask about prices (*C'est combien ?*, *Ça coûte combien ?*, *Quel est le prix ?*) and how to understand spoken totals.
- Requesting the check (*l'addition* vs *la facture*) and navigating payment methods (*par carte*, *sans contact*, *en espèces / comptant*).
- Cultural differences in tipping (*le pourboire*) and service charges between France and Canada.
- TCF Canada Speaking Task 2 strategic roleplay blueprint for leading a counter interaction.

### Polite ordering formulas and the magic of je voudrais

In English-speaking culture, it is common to walk up to a counter and say "I want a coffee" or "Give me a croissant." In the French-speaking world, direct language of this kind sounds aggressive and disrespectful.

#### The Golden Rule: Never Say "Je veux"

The verb *vouloir* (to want) conjugated in the present tense—**je veux** (/ʒə vø/)—expresses a blunt demand, similar to a demanding child saying "I want!" Adults never use *je veux* when ordering food or service.

Instead, French speakers use the **conditional of politeness** (*le conditionnel de politesse*):

> **Je voudrais...** — /ʒə vu.dʁɛ/ — *I would like...*

Compare these interactions:

> **Unacceptable**: *Je veux un café.* (/ʒə vø œ̃ ka.fe/) — Sounds rude and demanding.  
> **Polite**: **Bonjour monsieur, je voudrais un café au lait, s'il vous plaît.**  
> /bɔ̃.ʒuʁ mə.sjø | ʒə vu.dʁɛ œ̃ ka.fe o lɛ | sil vu plɛ/  
> *Hello sir, I would like a coffee with milk, please.*

#### Other Authentic Ordering Phrases

Depending on the setting and your level of formality, here are four other standard ways to place your order:

1. **Pour moi, ce sera...** (/puʁ mwa | sə sə.ʁa/) — *For me, it will be...*  
   Extremely natural when ordering at a table or following a dining companion:  
   > **Pour moi, ce sera une quiche lorraine avec une salade verte.**  
   > *For me, it will be a quiche lorraine with a green salad.*

2. **Est-ce que je peux avoir... ?** (/ɛs kə ʒə pø.z‿a.vwaʁ/) — *May I have...?*  
   > **Est-ce que je peux avoir un verre d'eau, s'il vous plaît ?**  
   > *May I have a glass of water, please?*

3. **Je vais prendre...** (/ʒə ve pʁɑ̃dʁ/) — *I am going to take / I'll have...*  
   Using the near future (*futur proche*):  
   > **Bonjour, je vais prendre la formule sandwich à midi.**  
   > *Hello, I'll have the sandwich combo at noon.*

4. **S'il vous plaît vs S'il te plaît**:  
   Always use **s'il vous plaît** (/sil vu plɛ/) with waitstaff, baristas, and cashiers. Using informal *s'il te plaît* with a service worker is an improper breach of social distance.

### Inquiring about prices and understanding totals

On the TCF Canada exam, asking and understanding prices is a core competency tested in both listening dialogues and interactive speaking simulations.

#### How to Ask the Price

There are three interchangeable questions for asking the cost of an item or service:

1. **C'est combien ?** (/s‿ɛ kɔ̃.bjɛ̃/) — *How much is it?* (Most common in daily spoken French)  
   > **Excusez-moi, c'est combien le croissant aux amandes ?**  
   > /ɛk.sky.ze mwa | s‿ɛ kɔ̃.bjɛ̃ lə kʁwa.sɑ̃ o.z‿a.mɑ̃d/  
   > *Excuse me, how much is the almond croissant?*

2. **Ça coûte combien ?** (/sa kut kɔ̃.bjɛ̃/) — *How much does that cost?*  
   > **Ça coûte combien pour un grand café et deux brioches ?**  
   > /sa kut kɔ̃.bjɛ̃ puʁ œ̃ ɡʁɑ̃ ka.fe e dø bʁi.jɔʃ/  
   > *How much does it cost for a large coffee and two brioches?*

3. **Quel est le prix de... ?** (/kɛ.l‿ɛ lə pʁi də/) — *What is the price of...?* (Slightly more formal)  
   > **Quel est le prix du menu du jour ?**  
   > /kɛ.l‿ɛ lə pʁi dy mə.ny dy ʒuʁ/  
   > *What is the price of the daily special?*

#### How Prices Are Expressed

When the cashier or server gives you the total, they will use one of three structures:

> **Ça fait...** (/sa fɛ/) — *That makes / That comes to...*  
> **Ça coûte...** (/sa kut/) — *That costs...*  
> **C'est...** (/s‿ɛ/) — *It is...*

In spoken French, the word for currency (*euros* in Europe, *dollars* in Canada) is often stated, but the word for cents (*centimes* in France, *cents* /sɛnt/ in Canada) is frequently omitted:

- **4,50 €** is pronounced:  
  *Quatre euros cinquante* (/katʁ.z‿ø.ʁo sɛ̃.kɑ̃t/)  
  *(Four euros fifty)*
- **12,75 $** is pronounced:  
  *Douze dollars soixante-quinze* (/duz dɔ.laʁ swa.sɑ̃t.kɛ̃z/)  
  *(Twelve dollars seventy-five)*
- **0,80 €** is pronounced:  
  *Quatre-vingts centimes* (/katʁ.vɛ̃ sɑ̃.tim/)  
  *(Eighty cents)*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Listening Trap: Decimal Separator Differences

In French orthography, decimal numbers use a **comma** (*la virgule*), not a period:
- English: `$4.50`
- French: `4,50 $` or `4,50 €`

When listening to fast native audio on the TCF, train your ears for the vigesimal number compounds you learned in Section 1: *soixante-dix* (70), *quatre-vingts* (80), and *quatre-vingt-dix* (90). A price like *quatre-vingt-quatorze euros* (94 €) is a classic distractor test!

</div>

### Payment methods, tipping, and transaction etiquette

Once your order is complete, you must settle the bill. The terminology differs slightly between European and Canadian French:

#### 1. Asking for the Bill

- **In France & Europe**:  
  > **L'addition, s'il vous plaît !** — /l‿a.di.sjɔ̃ sil vu plɛ/  
  > *The check / bill, please!*
- **In Canada & Québec**:  
  > **La facture, s'il vous plaît !** — /la fak.tyʁ sil vu plɛ/  
  > *The bill, please!*

#### 2. Specifying Your Payment Method

The server will ask: *"Comment désirez-vous régler ?"* or *"Vous payez comment ?"* (How are you paying?). Respond with:

- **Par carte (bancaire)** (/paʁ kaʁt bɑ̃.kɛʁ/) — *By debit/credit card.*
- **Sans contact** (/sɑ̃ kɔ̃.takt/) — *Contactless / Tap.*
- **En espèces** (/ɑ̃.n‿ɛs.pɛs/) or **En liquide** (/ɑ̃ li.kid/) — *In cash (France).*
- **En argent comptant** (/ɑ̃.n‿aʁ.ʒɑ̃ kɔ̃.tɑ̃/) — *In cash (Canada).*

If paying cash and waiting for your change:
- *La monnaie* (/la mɔ.nɛ/) = *The coins / the change returned*.
- If you don't need change: **Gardez la monnaie !** (/ɡaʁ.de la mɔ.nɛ/) — *Keep the change!*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France: Tipping Culture (*Le pourboire*)

One of the largest cultural shocks between France and Canada involves tipping:

- **In France**: By law, every restaurant, café, and bar bill includes a 15% service charge built directly into the menu prices (**Service compris**). Leaving a tip is entirely optional and modest: leaving 1 to 2 euros in small change (*un pourboire*) on the table is considered polite for good service, but leaving 0 euros is not an insult.
- **In Québec and Canada**: Service is **not included**. Waitstaff are paid a lower tipped minimum wage, and customers are culturally and economically expected to add a tip (**le pourboire** **(QC)**) of **15% to 20%** before sales tax. When paying by card machine, the payment terminal will explicitly prompt you to select 15%, 18%, or 20%. Failing to tip in Canada is considered a serious breach of social conduct.

Other Canadian terms to remember:
- **Une chocolatine** **(QC)**: While in Paris you ask for *un pain au chocolat*, in Montreal, Quebec City, and South-West France you must order *une chocolatine* **(QC)**.
- **Un breuvage** **(QC)**: On Canadian menus, the beverage section is headed *Breuvages* **(QC)** instead of European *Boissons*.
- **La facture** **(QC)**: Never ask for *l'addition* in a Quebec diner; ask for *la facture*.

#### Exam Tip: TCF Speaking Task 2 Roleplay Strategy

In **Speaking Task 2 (*Exercice en interaction*)**, the examiner gives you a prompt card (e.g., *"Vous êtes dans un nouveau café à Québec. Vous posez des questions sur le menu, les formules petit-déjeuner et les prix"*). You have **2 minutes of preparation** and **3 minutes 30 seconds of roleplay**.

To maximize your score on the FEI rubric:
1. **Take the initiative**: You must lead the conversation. Greet the examiner formally: *"Bonjour monsieur / madame, excusez-moi de vous déranger..."*
2. **Do not just say "I want X"**: Ask questions! Use interrogatives:
   - *"Qu'est-ce que vous proposez comme formule pour le petit-déjeuner ?"*
   - *"Est-ce que le café au lait est compris dans le menu ?"*
   - *"Est-ce que vous avez des options végétariennes ?"*
   - *"Combien coûte la quiche du jour ?"*
   - *"Est-ce que vous acceptez les cartes de crédit ou seulement l'argent comptant ?"*
3. **React to answers**: Don't just fire off a checklist. If the examiner says they are out of croissants, react naturally: *"Ah, c'est dommage ! Dans ce cas, je vais prendre une brioche."*
