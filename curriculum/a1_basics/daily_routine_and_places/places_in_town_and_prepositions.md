### Introduction

Navigating a new city is one of the most immediate challenges facing any newcomer in Canada. From locating the nearest pharmacy to finding the immigration service center, catching the right bus, or visiting your local medical clinic, you need the language of physical space. In French, asking for and understanding directions is not just a matter of listing building names; it requires mastering the precise logic of spatial prepositions, directional verbs, and the unique French concept of *chez*.

On the TCF Canada exam, spatial navigation and city geography feature prominently in two key areas. In the *Compréhension orale* (Listening) section, early, easier items may feature brief dialogues where speakers ask for directions or describe where an event will take place, using spatial distractors (*en face de* versus *à côté de*, *à droite* versus *à gauche*) to test your attention to detail. In the *Compréhension écrite* (Reading) section, you may read municipal public transit notices and local directory listings.

In this lesson, you will acquire high-frequency vocabulary for city landmarks and public services, master static location prepositions and their mandatory contractions with *de* (*près du*, *en face de la*), learn how to navigate someone to a destination using directional phrases, master the cultural preposition *chez*, and explore Canadian city layout conventions, from Montreal's *dépanneurs* to bilingual street signage.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Essential vocabulary for city landmarks, public institutions, and transit hubs.
- Spatial prepositions of location: **dans**, **sur**, **devant**, **derrière**, **entre**, **à côté de**, **en face de**, and **près de**.
- Mandatory contractions of prepositions with the definite article (*de + le* = *du*, *de + les* = *des*).
- The unique preposition **chez** used with people, residences, and professionals (*chez le médecin*, *chez moi*).
- Directional verbs and movement phrases: **aller tout droit**, **tourner à gauche**, **tourner à droite**, **traverser**.
- Canadian urban geography terms, including street numbering (*Est / Ouest*) and Québec's iconic **dépanneur** **(QC)**.
- TCF listening strategies for identifying locations and eliminating directional distractors.

When exploring a Francophone city like Montréal, Québec, or Ottawa, you will regularly interact with a core set of municipal, commercial, and transport buildings.

Observe how native speakers identify places in town:

> **La bibliothèque municipale est ouverte aujourd'hui.**  
> /la bi.bli.jɔ.tɛk my.ni.si.pal ɛ.t‿u.vɛʁt o.ʒuʁ.dɥi/

### Spatial prepositions and location markers

To describe where an object or building is situated relative to another, French uses specific prepositions of place. Some prepositions consist of a single word, while compound prepositions end with **de**.

Observe the contrast between simple and compound spatial prepositions:

> **La voiture est devant la maison.** — /la vwa.tyʁ ɛ də.vɑ̃ la mɛ.zɔ̃/  
> *The car is in front of the house.*

> **Le restaurant est en face de la gare.** — /lə ʁɛs.to.ʁɑ̃ ɛ.t‿ɑ̃ fas də la ɡaʁ/  
> *The restaurant is across from (facing) the train station.*

> **L'école se trouve entre le parc et la bibliothèque.**  
> /le.kɔl sə tʁuv ɑ̃tʁ lə paʁk e la bi.bli.jɔ.tɛk/  
> *The school is located between the park and the library.*

#### 1. Simple Spatial Prepositions (No "de")
These prepositions connect directly to the noun and its article:

- **dans** (/dɑ̃/) — inside / in: *dans le bureau* (in the office)
- **sur** (/syʁ/) — on / on top of: *sur la table* (on the table)
- **sous** (/su/) — under / underneath: *sous le pont* (under the bridge)
- **devant** (/də.vɑ̃/) — in front of: *devant la banque* (in front of the bank)
- **derrière** (/dɛ.ʁjɛʁ/) — behind: *derrière l'école* (behind the school)
- **entre** (/ɑ̃tʁ/) — between: *entre deux immeubles* (between two buildings)

#### 2. Compound Spatial Prepositions with Mandatory Contraction
When a preposition ends in **de**, that *de* **must contract** with the definite article of the following noun:
- **de + le** = **du**
- **de + la** = **de la** (no contraction)
- **de + l'** = **de l'** (no contraction before vowel)
- **de + les** = **des**

Observe these mandatory contractions in action:

| Compound Preposition | Before Masc. Sing. (*le*) | Before Fem. Sing. (*la*) | Before Vowel (*l'*) | Before Plural (*les*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **à côté de** (next to) | **à côté du** parc | **à côté de la** poste | **à côté de l'**hôpital | **à côté des** magasins |
| **en face de** (across from) | **en face du** musée | **en face de la** gare | **en face de l'**école | **en face des** banques |
| **près de** (near / close to) | **près du** métro | **près de la** mairie | **près de l'**arrêt | **près des** restaurants |
| **loin de** (far from) | **loin du** centre | **loin de la** ville | **loin de l'**aéroport | **loin des** bureaux |
| **au coin de** (at the corner of) | **au coin du** boulevard | **au coin de la** rue | **au coin de l'**avenue | **au coin des** rues |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Grammar Trap: Never Say "Près de le"

In French, writing or saying *"près de le parc"* or *"à côté de le supermarché"* is an immediate grammatical error. Whenever *de* meets *le*, it **must** contract to **du**:
- ✗ *Le restaurant est à côté de le cinéma.*
- ✓ **Le restaurant est à côté du cinéma.**
- ✗ *J'habite en face de les magasins.*
- ✓ **J'habite en face des magasins.**

</div>

### The preposition chez: Visiting people and professionals

One of the most characteristic and useful prepositions in the French language is **chez** (/ʃe/). English has no direct one-word equivalent. Depending on context, *chez* means "at or to the home of," "at the office/shop of a professional," or "in the country/culture of."

Observe how native speakers use **chez**:

> **Ce soir, je dîne chez des amis.** — /sə swaʁ, ʒə din ʃe de.z‿a.mi/  
> *Tonight, I am having dinner at friends' place.*

> **Je dois aller chez le médecin à quatorze heures.**  
> /ʒə dwa.z‿a.le ʃe lə mɛd.sɛ̃ a ka.tɔʁz œʁ/  
> *I have to go to the doctor's (office) at 2:00 pm.*

> **Après le travail, je rentre chez moi.** — /a.pʁɛ lə tʁa.vaj, ʒə ʁɑ̃tʁ ʃe mwa/  
> *After work, I am going back home.*

#### Rules for Using Chez:
1. **Chez + Person / Name**: Use *chez* when referring to a person's residence or presence:
   - *chez Marie* (at Marie's house)
   - *chez mes parents* (at my parents' house)
2. **Chez + Stress Pronoun**: Expressing "my place / your place / their place":
   - **chez moi** (/ʃe mwa/) — at my place / home
   - **chez toi** (/ʃe twa/) — at your place (informal)
   - **chez lui / chez elle** (/ʃe lɥi/, /ʃe ɛl/) — at his place / her place
   - **chez nous** (/ʃe nu/) — at our place / home
   - **chez vous** (/ʃe vu/) — at your place (formal/plural)
   - **chez eux / chez elles** (/ʃe.z‿ø/, /ʃe.z‿ɛl/) — at their place
3. **Chez + Profession (Person) vs À + Building (Place)**:
   - When referring to the professional person, use **chez**:  
     *Je vais **chez le médecin** / **chez le dentiste** / **chez le coiffeur**.* (at the doctor's / dentist's / hairdresser's)
   - When referring to the physical institution or building, use **à**:  
     *Je vais **à l'hôpital** / **à la clinique** / **au salon de coiffure**.*

### Giving and understanding street directions

When asking for directions in a Francophone city, you will use formal polite formulas. In response, locals will provide a sequence of directional commands.

Observe this authentic street dialogue:

> **Pardon madame, pour aller à la station de métro, s'il vous plaît ?**  
> *Excuse me, ma'am, how do I get to the subway station, please?*  
> **C'est très simple : vous allez tout droit jusqu'au feu, puis vous tournez à gauche sur la rue Saint-Denis. La station est en face du café.**  
> *It's very simple: you go straight until the traffic light, then you turn left onto Saint-Denis Street. The station is across from the cafe.*

#### Essential Directional Vocabulary:

| French Direction | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Allez tout droit** | /a.le tu dʁwa/ | Go straight ahead | Note: *droit* (straight) vs *droite* (right) |
| **Tournez à droite** | /tuʁ.ne a dʁwat/ | Turn right | Audible final /t/ sound |
| **Tournez à gauche** | /tuʁ.ne a ɡoʃ/ | Turn left | Clear /o/ sound |
| **Traversez la rue** | /tʁa.vɛʁ.se la ʁy/ | Cross the street | Regular *-er* verb |
| **Prenez la première rue à droite** | /pʁə.ne la pʁə.mjɛʁ ʁy a dʁwat/ | Take the first street on the right | High-frequency direction instruction |
| **Prenez la deuxième rue à gauche** | /pʁə.ne la dø.zjɛm ʁy a ɡoʃ/ | Take the second street on the left | Ordinal numbers: *deuxième* |
| **Jusqu'au feu / carrefour** | /ʒys.ko fø / kaʁ.fuʁ/ | Until the traffic light / intersection | *jusque + à + le* = *jusqu'au* |
| **C'est au bout de la rue** | /sɛ.t‿o bu də la ʁy/ | It is at the end of the street | *bout* = physical end |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Phonetic Trap: Tout droit vs À droite

English speakers frequently confuse these two crucial directions:
- **Tout droit** (/tu dʁwa/): The final *t* is silent! It means **straight ahead**.
- **À droite** (/a dʁwat/): The feminine ending makes the final *t* distinctly **audible** (/dʁwat/)! It means **to the right**.

If you say `/a.le tu dʁwat/`, a French listener will understand "go to the right" instead of "go straight"! This distinction is a classic listening trap.

</div>

### Canadian city geography: Streets, landmarks, and depanneurs

In Canada, urban layouts and regional vocabulary present distinct characteristics that differ from European Francophone cities.

#### 1. The Boulevard Saint-Laurent Divide (Est vs Ouest)
In Montréal, the city is historically divided into **Est** (East) and **Ouest** (West) by Boulevard Saint-Laurent. Street addresses explicitly state the orientation:
- *1250, rue Sainte-Catherine **Ouest***
- *450, rue Sherbrooke **Est***

When asking for directions in Montreal, knowing whether an address is *Est* or *Ouest* is critical, as numbers start at zero at Boulevard Saint-Laurent and increase in both directions.

#### 2. The Iconic Dépanneur
In Québec, the small neighborhood corner convenience store (selling milk, bread, snacks, beverages, and daily essentials) is universally known as a **dépanneur** **(QC)** (often abbreviated colloquially as *le dép*):
- > **Je descends au dépanneur acheter du lait.** **(QC)**  
  > *I'm going down to the convenience store to buy milk.*

In France, people would refer to this as *une supérette* or *une épicerie de quartier*.

#### 3. Transit Vocabulary
In Canada, you will encounter these standard transit terms on signage and schedules:
- **un autobus** (/œ̃.n‿o.to.bys/) or **un bus** — city bus
- **une station de métro** (/yn sta.sjɔ̃ də me.tʁo/) — subway station
- **un arrêt d'autobus** (/œ̃.n‿a.ʁɛ do.to.bys/) — bus stop
- **une voie cyclable / piste cyclable** — bike lane / path

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In France, a parking lot or parking space is commonly referred to by the English loanword *un parking*. In Québec, the Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF) has firmly established the standard French term **un stationnement** **(QC)**:
- *Le stationnement est gratuit pour les clients.* **(QC)** (The parking is free for customers.)
On the TCF Canada exam, Canadian speakers are likely to say *stationnement*, but be ready to recognize *parking* too.

</div>
