### Introduction

In French, regular verbs follow predictable formulas, but the engine of daily conversation is driven by a small group of irregular power verbs. After *être* (to be) and *avoir* (to have), three verbs dominate nearly every social exchange, practical errand, and physical movement: **aller** (to go), **faire** (to do / to make), and **venir** (to come).

These three verbs are not simply vocabulary items; they are structural building blocks that unlock fundamental grammatical timeframes and cultural idioms. With **aller**, you will describe where you are heading, inquire about health, and form the **futur proche** (immediate future: *I am going to leave*). With **faire**, you will describe the weather, narrate sports and household activities, and talk about your job. With **venir**, you will state your geographic origins and form the **passé récent** (immediate past: *I just arrived*).

For the TCF Canada candidate, mastering this triad marks the transition from static, one-clause sentences to dynamic temporal narration. In **Speaking Task 1** (*Entretien dirigé*) and **Writing Task 1**, candidates who can move seamlessly between present actions, upcoming plans with *aller*, and recent events with *venir* immediately demonstrate the grammatical flexibility expected of a solid A2 speaker and beyond.

### The verb aller: Movement, health, and the futur proche

The verb **aller** (to go) is unique in French linguistics. Although its infinitive ends in *-er*, it belongs to the irregular third group because its stems across tenses derive from three completely different historical Latin roots (*vais*, *allons*, *ira*).

Observe how native speakers conjugate and pronounce **aller**:

> **Je vais au travail à pied.** — /ʒə vɛ o tʁa.vaj a pje/  
> *I go to work on foot.*

> **Comment allez-vous ? — Je vais très bien, merci.** — /kɔ.mɑ̃.t‿a.le vu ? — ʒə vɛ tʁɛ bjɛ̃, mɛʁ.si/  
> *How are you? — I am doing very well, thank you.*

> **Nous allons au centre-ville cet après-midi.** — /nu.z‿a.lɔ̃ o sɑ̃tʁ.vil sɛ.t‿a.pʁɛ.mi.di/  
> *We are going downtown this afternoon.*

Here is the complete present tense conjugation of **aller**:

| Subject Pronoun | Conjugated Form | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Je** | **vais** | /ʒə vɛ/ | I go / I am going |
| **Tu** | **vas** | /ty va/ | You go (informal) |
| **Il / Elle / On** | **va** | /il va / ɛl va / ɔ̃ va/ | He / She / One goes |
| **Nous** | **allons** | /nu.z‿a.lɔ̃/ | We go (mandatory liaison: /z/) |
| **Vous** | **allez** | /vu.z‿a.le/ | You go (mandatory liaison: /z/) |
| **Ils / Elles** | **vont** | /il vɔ̃ / ɛl vɔ̃/ | They go (nasal vowel /ɔ̃/) |

Notice that the final consonants in *vais*, *vas*, and *vont* are silent. Pay special attention to the 3rd person plural **ils vont** (/il vɔ̃/): do not confuse it with **ils sont** (/il sɔ̃/, from *être*) or **ils ont** (/il.z‿ɔ̃/, from *avoir*).

#### Expressing the Immediate Future: Le Futur Proche
One of the most practical grammatical tools in the French language is the **futur proche** (immediate or near future). Instead of memorizing complex future tense endings, you simply combine the present tense of **aller** with any **infinitive verb**:

$$\text{Sujet} + \text{aller (présent)} + \text{verbe à l'infinitif}$$

Observe how naturally this expresses upcoming events and intentions:

> **Je vais déménager à Montréal le mois prochain.**  
> /ʒə vɛ de.me.na.ʒe a mɔ̃.ʁe.al lə mwa pʁɔ.ʃɛ̃/  
> *I am going to move to Montreal next month.*

> **Nous allons visiter la ville ce week-end.**  
> /nu.z‿a.lɔ̃ vi.zi.te la vil sə wi.kɛnd/  
> *We are going to visit the city this weekend.*

> **Elle va commencer un nouvel emploi lundi.**  
> /ɛl va kɔ.mɑ̃.se œ̃ nu.vɛl ɑ̃.plwa lœ̃.di/  
> *She is going to start a new job on Monday.*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--warning" markdown="1">

#### Exam tip

In TCF Canada Speaking Task 1, after you introduce yourself, the examiner may ask: *"Quels sont vos projets d'avenir ?"* ("What are your plans for the future?") or *"Pourquoi voulez-vous immigrer au Canada ?"*. Use the *futur proche* to deliver crisp, fluent responses: **"Je vais travailler dans mon domaine et ma famille va s'installer au Québec."** This demonstrates temporal competence without stumbling over complex conjugations.

### The verb faire: Activities, weather, and daily expressions

The verb **faire** translates broadly to both "to do" and "to make." Beyond its literal meanings, *faire* acts as a versatile idiomatic engine in French.

Observe the conjugations in context:

> **Qu'est-ce que vous faites dans la vie ?** — /kɛs.kə vu fɛt dɑ̃ la vi/  
> *What do you do for a living? (Common interview and exam question)*

> **Je fais du vélo le samedi matin.** — /ʒə fɛ dy ve.lo lə sam.di ma.tɛ̃/  
> *I go cycling on Saturday mornings.*

> **Ils font leurs devoirs à la bibliothèque.** — /il fɔ̃ lœʁ də.vwaʁ a la bi.bli.jɔ.tɛk/  
> *They do their homework at the library.*

Here is the present tense conjugation of **faire**:

| Subject Pronoun | Conjugated Form | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Je** | **fais** | /ʒə fɛ/ | I do / I make |
| **Tu** | **fais** | /ty fɛ/ | You do (informal) |
| **Il / Elle / On** | **fait** | /il fɛ / ɛl fɛ / ɔ̃ fɛ/ | He / She / One does |
| **Nous** | **faisons** | /nu fə.zɔ̃/ | We do (pronounced /fə.zɔ̃/, NOT /fɛ.zɔ̃/!) |
| **Vous** | **faites** | /vu fɛt/ | You do (formal/plural — irregular ending!) |
| **Ils / Elles** | **font** | /il fɔ̃ / ɛl fɔ̃/ | They do / They make |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Grammar Trap: Vous faites and Nous faisons

Two forms of *faire* catch even advanced learners off guard:
1. **Vous faites** (/vu fɛt/): *Faire* does **not** end in *-ez* with *vous*! Saying *"vous faisez"* is an immediate grammatical error. (Only three French verbs end in *-tes* with *vous*: *vous êtes*, *vous faites*, *vous dites*).
2. **Nous faisons** (/nu fə.zɔ̃/): Despite the spelling *ai*, the stem is pronounced with a neutral vowel /ə/ (/nu fə.zɔ̃/), not /fɛ.zɔ̃/.

</div>

#### 1. Talking About the Weather with "Il fait"
In French, you never use *être* to describe weather temperatures or conditions. You use the impersonal pronoun **il** followed by **fait**:

| Weather Expression | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Il fait beau.** | /il fɛ bo/ | The weather is nice / sunny. |
| **Il fait chaud.** | /il fɛ ʃo/ | It is hot. |
| **Il fait froid.** | /il fɛ fʁwa/ | It is cold. |
| **Il fait frais.** | /il fɛ fʁɛ/ | It is cool / chilly. |
| **Il fait mauvais.** | /il fɛ mɔ.vɛ/ | The weather is bad. |
| **Il fait du vent.** | /il fɛ dy vɑ̃/ | It is windy. |
| **Il fait du soleil.** | /il fɛ dy sɔ.lɛj/ | It is sunny. |

#### 2. Hobbies, Sports, and Daily Activities
When describing recreational activities with *faire*, French pairs *faire* with the partitive articles (**du**, **de la**, **de l'**):
- **faire du sport** (/fɛʁ dy spɔʁ/) — to do sports / exercise
- **faire du vélo** (/fɛʁ dy ve.lo/) — to cycle
- **faire de la natation** (/fɛʁ də la na.ta.sjɔ̃/) — to swim
- **faire de la randonnée** (/fɛʁ də la ʁɑ̃.dɔ.ne/) — to go hiking
- **faire la cuisine** (/fɛʁ la kɥi.zin/) — to cook / do the cooking
- **faire le ménage** (/fɛʁ lə me.naʒ/) — to clean the house / do housework
- **faire les courses** (/fɛʁ le kuʁs/) — to go grocery shopping

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In France, buying everyday groceries is called **faire les courses**. In Québec and across French Canada, the standard expression is **faire l'épicerie** **(QC)**:
- *Je vais faire l'épicerie au supermarché.* **(QC)**
Furthermore, in Canadian winters, when temperatures drop drastically, locals describe extreme cold with the colloquial term **il fait frette** (/il fɛ fʁɛt/) **(QC)** instead of *il fait très froid*. While *frette* is casual, *faire l'épicerie* is standard Canadian French.

</div>

### The verb venir: Origins, departures, and the passe recent

The verb **venir** translates to "to come." It describes physical arrival, geographic origin, and temporal immediacy.

Observe the conjugations in context:

> **Je viens du Maroc et j'habite à Québec.** — /ʒə vjɛ̃ dy ma.ʁɔk e ʒa.bit a ke.bɛk/  
> *I come from Morocco and I live in Quebec City.*

> **D'où venez-vous ?** — /du və.ne vu/  
> *Where do you come from? (Formal origin inquiry)*

> **Ils viennent de commencer la réunion.** — /il vjɛn də kɔ.mɑ̃.se la ʁe.y.njɔ̃/  
> *They just started the meeting.*

Here is the present tense conjugation of **venir**:

| Subject Pronoun | Conjugated Form | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Je** | **viens** | /ʒə vjɛ̃/ | I come |
| **Tu** | **viens** | /ty vjɛ̃/ | You come (informal) |
| **Il / Elle / On** | **vient** | /il vjɛ̃ / ɛl vjɛ̃ / ɔ̃ vjɛ̃/ | He / She / One comes |
| **Nous** | **venons** | /nu və.nɔ̃/ | We come |
| **Vous** | **venez** | /vu və.ne/ | You come (formal/plural) |
| **Ils / Elles** | **viennent** | /il vjɛn / ɛl vjɛn/ | They come (doubled *n*, oral /ɛn/) |

Notice the phonetic contrast between singular forms (*viens/vient*: nasal vowel **/jɛ̃/**) and the third-person plural (*viennent*: oral open vowel plus audible consonant **/jɛn/**).

#### 1. Expressing Origin: Venir de
To express where you or your family come from, combine **venir** with the preposition **de** (*du* for masculine countries, *de* with no article for feminine countries, *d'* before a vowel, *des* for plural countries):
- **Je viens du Sénégal.** (masculine country: *de + le* = *du*)
- **Elle vient de France.** (feminine country: *de*)
- **Nous venons d'Algérie.** (vowel-initial country: *d'*)
- **Ils viennent des États-Unis.** (plural country: *de + les* = *des*)

#### 2. Expressing the Immediate Past: Le Passé Récent
Just as *aller + infinitive* expresses what is about to happen, **venir de + infinitive** expresses an action completed just moments ago (equivalent to English "to have just done something"):

$$\text{Sujet} + \text{venir (présent)} + \text{de (ou d')} + \text{verbe à l'infinitif}$$

Observe how easily this conveys recent events:

> **Je viens de terminer mon café.**  
> /ʒə vjɛ̃ də tɛʁ.mi.ne mɔ̃ ka.fe/  
> *I just finished my coffee.*

> **Le train vient d'arriver en gare.**  
> /lə tʁɛ̃ vjɛ̃ da.ʁi.ve ɑ̃ ɡaʁ/  
> *The train just arrived at the station.*

> **Nous venons de signer notre bail à Gatineau.**  
> /nu və.nɔ̃ də si.ɲe nɔtʁ baj a ɡa.ti.no/  
> *We just signed our lease in Gatineau.*

### Synthesizing the triad: A complete day of actions

Notice how combining regular *-er* verbs with *aller*, *faire*, and *venir* creates rich, cohesive communicative paragraphs suitable for TCF Canada Writing Task 1:

> **Ce matin, je viens d'arriver au bureau. Aujourd'hui, il fait beau mais un peu frais. À midi, je vais manger avec mes collègues dans un petit restaurant près du parc. L'après-midi, nous faisons le point en réunion d'équipe. Ce soir, je vais faire l'épicerie et je vais préparer le souper avec ma conjointe.**  
> *This morning, I just arrived at the office. Today, the weather is nice but a bit cool. At noon, I am going to eat with my colleagues in a small restaurant near the park. In the afternoon, we take stock in a team meeting. Tonight, I am going to do the grocery shopping and prepare dinner with my partner.*

Observe the variety of structures:
- Immediate past: *je viens d'arriver*
- Weather idiom: *il fait beau mais un peu frais*
- Near future: *je vais manger*, *je vais faire*, *je vais préparer*
- Activity idiom: *nous faisons le point*, *faire l'épicerie*
