### Introduction

In the computerized version of the TCF Canada (*TCF sur ordinateur* or *TCF-SO*), the **Expression écrite** section poses an unexpected and devastating obstacle for many candidates: **typing in French under strict time limits without spellcheck, autocorrect, or word completion**.

In daily life, smartphones, email clients, and word processors automatically capitalize initial letters, insert acute and grave accents, insert apostrophes, and correct minor typos. When you sit in an accredited exam center in Toronto, Montreal, Paris, Dubai, or Dakar, however, the test delivery software provides only a stark text area and an on-screen accent toolbar. If you must use your mouse to click on an on-screen button every time you need an **é**, an **à**, a **ç**, or an **ê**, you will lose between **four and seven minutes** across the 60-minute writing test. That lost time is often the difference between completing the crucial Task 3 synthesis or being cut off mid-paragraph.

Furthermore, France Éducation international (FEI) examiners enforce strict morphological penalties: omitting an accent on **« a »** versus **« à »**, or writing **« mange »** instead of **« mangé »**, changes the grammatical identity of the word and degrades your score on the *Correction morphosyntaxique* criterion.

This lesson equips you with the exact hardware setups, operating system keyboard layouts, dead-key muscle memory, and typing benchmarks required to type at least **25 to 35 words per minute in French** on standard physical keyboards without looking at your fingers or reaching for the mouse.

### The exam center keyboard reality: Hardware, layouts, and zero-autocorrect

Before entering the examination room, you must know what physical setup awaits you. Test centers worldwide generally fall into three geographic hardware categories:

1. **North America (English Canada and the United States)**:
   - Physical keyboards are standard **QWERTY** (ANSI layout).
   - In Canadian test centers, the operating system is typically configured to either **US-International** or **Canadian Multilingual Standard (CMS)**.
2. **Québec and Francophone Canada**:
   - Keyboards often feature bilingual Canadian physical keycaps, using the **Canadian Multilingual Standard** (often referred to as *clavier canadien-français*).
3. **France, Belgium, and North/West Africa**:
   - Physical keyboards are almost exclusively **AZERTY** (French standard layout).
4. **Other International Centers (Latin America, Asia, Middle East, Europe non-Francophone)**:
   - Physical keyboards are standard **QWERTY**, with Windows or Linux operating systems set to **US-International** or providing an on-screen accent palette.

While this palette is a helpful backup for rare characters like **« œ »**, relying on it for everyday vowels will cripple your pacing. Typing 350 words across the three tasks with ~70 accented characters means **70 mouse interruptions**. You must type all accents directly from the physical keyboard.

#### The Zero-Autocorrect Standard

The test interface runs in a locked-down browser environment (such as Safe Exam Browser or a proprietary FEI kiosk application). Under these conditions:
- **No red squiggly lines**: Misspelled words are not underlined.
- **No auto-capitalization**: Starting a new sentence after a period requires holding the `Shift` key.
- **No auto-apostrophe**: Typing `jai` will remain `jai`; you must actively type **« j'ai »**.
- **No predictive text**: No word completions or grammar suggestions are available.

If you practice your daily writing assignments in Google Docs, Microsoft Word, or Apple Notes with autocorrect enabled, your practice is artificially inflated. You must complete all exam writing drills in a plain text editor with spellcheck completely disabled.

### Accent muscle memory: US-International, Canadian Multilingual, and AZERTY

To achieve effortless typing speed, you must train muscle memory for the specific keyboard layout you will use on test day.

#### 1. The QWERTY US-International Layout (Recommended for Most Learners)

The **US-International** layout transforms certain punctuation keys (`'`, `"`, `` ` ``, `~`, `^`) into **dead keys**. A dead key does not produce a character immediately; it modifies the vowel typed immediately after it.

| Target Accent | Dead Key Combination | Example Character | Keyboard Keystrokes |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Accent aigu ( ´ )** | Apostrophe `'` + Vowel | **é** | Press `'` then `e` |
| **Accent grave ( ` )** | Backtick `` ` `` + Vowel | **à**, **è**, **ù** | Press `` ` `` then `a`, `e`, or `u` |
| **Accent circonflexe ( ^ )** | Caret `Shift + 6` + Vowel | **â**, **ê**, **î**, **ô**, **û** | Press `Shift + 6` then the vowel |
| **Tréma ( ¨ )** | Quotation mark `Shift + '` + Vowel | **ë**, **ï** | Press `Shift + '` then `e` or `i` |
| **Cédille ( ç )** | Apostrophe `'` + `c` (or `AltGr + ,`) | **ç** | Press `'` then `c` |
| **Apostrophe alone** | Apostrophe `'` + `Space` | **'** | Press `'` then `Space` |
| **Quotes alone** | `Shift + '` + `Space` | **"** | Press `Shift + '` then `Space` |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### The Spacebar Reflex for Apostrophes

The most common frustration for beginners using US-International is typing an elided word like **« j'ai »** or **« c'est »**. If you type `j`, then `'`, then `a`, the dead key combines with the `a` to produce **« jà »**!

To produce a true apostrophe followed by a vowel:
- Press `'` then immediately tap the **Spacebar**, then type the vowel:
  - `j` + `'` + `Space` + `a` + `i` $\rightarrow$ **j'ai** — /ʒe/ — *I have*
  - `c` + `'` + `Space` + `e` + `s` + `t` $\rightarrow$ **c'est** — /sɛ/ — *it is*
  - `d` + `'` + `Space` + `a` + `c` + `c` + `o` + `r` + `d` $\rightarrow$ **d'accord** — /da.kɔʁ/ — *agreed*

Train this **apostrophe-spacebar-vowel** sequence until it becomes an automatic micro-reflex.

</div>

#### 2. The Canadian Multilingual Standard Layout (CMS)

If you test in Canada, the computers are frequently set to the **Canadian Multilingual Standard** (often indicated by a keyboard icon showing *CF* or *CMS* in the taskbar). CMS provides direct single-key access to the most common French characters on a physical QWERTY keyboard:

- **é**: Dedicated key located on the key immediately to the right of `l` (where `;` is on standard US QWERTY).
- **è**: Dedicated key located on the `'` key.
- **à**: Dedicated key located on the `\` key.
- **ç**: Dedicated key located on the `]` key.
- **^ (Circonflexe)**: Dedicated dead key on the `[` key. Press `[` followed by any vowel.
- **¨ (Tréma)**: Press `Shift + [` followed by any vowel.

The major advantage of CMS is that you do not need dead-key combinations for **é**, **è**, **à**, or **ç**. However, standard punctuation marks like `?`, `;`, and `:` require holding `Shift` or `AltGr`.

#### 3. The AZERTY French Standard Layout

If you test in France, Belgium, or certain African test centers, the keyboard physical layout changes fundamentally:
- The `A` and `Q` keys are swapped.
- The `Z` and `W` keys are swapped.
- The `M` key is located to the right of `L` (not on the bottom row).
- Numbers (`1`, `2`, `3`...) require holding the **Shift** key! The unshifted number keys produce accented letters directly:
  - `2` produces **é**
  - `7` produces **è**
  - `9` produces **ç**
  - `0` produces **à**
  - `ù` has its own dedicated key next to the Enter key.

If you are not accustomed to AZERTY, taking an exam on one without prior practice will reduce your typing speed by 70%. If your test center is in an AZERTY zone, you must practice on an AZERTY layout for at least two weeks before exam day.

### High-frequency typing traps: Elisions, hyphens, and grammatical accents

When writing under severe time constraints, typing mistakes easily transform into severe grammatical deductions. Examiners distinguish between a benign phonetic slip and a morphosyntactic error.

#### 1. The Accent that Changes the Part of Speech

Omitting an accent in French frequently turns a verb into a preposition, or an adverb into an article:

| Accented Form | Grammatical Identity | Unaccented Form | Grammatical Identity | Exemplary Exam Pair |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **à** | Preposition (*to, at*) | **a** | Auxiliary verb *avoir* (*has*) | **Il a voyagé à Montréal.** — /i.l‿a vwa.ja.ʒe a mɔ̃.ʁe.al/ — *He traveled to Montreal.* |
| **où** | Relative pronoun/adverb (*where*) | **ou** | Conjunction (*or*) | **La ville où nous habitons ou travaillons.** — /la vil u nu.z‿a.bi.tɔ̃ u tʁa.va.jɔ̃/ — *The city where we live or work.* |
| **la** / **le** | Definite article (*the*) | **là** | Adverb of place/time (*there*) | **Restez là pendant la réunion.** — /ʁɛs.te la pɑ̃.dɑ̃ la ʁe.y.njɔ̃/ — *Stay there during the meeting.* |
| **dû** | Past participle of *devoir* | **du** | Contracted article (*de + le*) | **Il a dû payer le prix du billet.** — /i.l‿a dy pe.je lə pʁi dy bi.jɛ/ — *He had to pay the price of the ticket.* |
| **sûr** | Adjective (*sure, certain*) | **sur** | Preposition (*on, upon*) | **Je suis sûr de la poser sur la table.** — /ʒə sɥi syʁ də la po.ze syʁ la tabl/ — *I am sure I put it on the table.* |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--warning" markdown="1">

#### Exam tip: Past participle vs Infinitive (-é vs -er)

The most heavily penalized spelling mistake in French is substituting the infinitive **-er** for the past participle **-é** (or vice versa):
- Incorrect: *« J'ai participer à la conférence. »*
- Correct: **« J'ai participé à la conférence. »** — /ʒe paʁ.ti.si.pe a la kɔ̃.fe.ʁɑ̃s/ — *I participated in the conference.*

Because both sound identical (/paʁ.ti.si.pe/), typing quickly without proofreading leads candidates to type `-er` out of habit. 

**The Replacement Test**: Always substitute a 3rd-group verb like **vendre** (/vɑ̃dʁ/) or **prendre** (/pʁɑ̃dʁ/):
- Can you say: *« J'ai prendre à la conférence »*? No. You say *« J'ai pris »* (past participle). Therefore, write the participle: **participé**.
- Can you say: *« Je veux prendre part »*? Yes (infinitive). Therefore, write the infinitive: **participer**.

</div>

#### 2. Mandatory Elisions and the Apostrophe

In French, elision (*l'élision*) is not optional; it is a mandatory phonetic rule. Failing to elide is treated as a foundational grammatical error:
- Incorrect: *« Je ai vu ce que il a fait. »*
- Correct: **« J'ai vu ce qu'il a fait. »** — /ʒe vy sə k‿i.l‿a fɛ/ — *I saw what he did.*

Words that must undergo elision before a vowel or mute h (*h muet*):
- **Articles**: *le*, *la* $\rightarrow$ **l'ami**, **l'heure**
- **Pronouns**: *je*, *me*, *te*, *se*, *le*, *la* $\rightarrow$ **j'arrive**, **il m'appelle**, **tu t'assieds**
- **Negative and conjunctions**: *ne*, *que* $\rightarrow$ **il n'écoute pas**, **bien qu'il sache**
- **Preposition**: *de* $\rightarrow$ **le cours d'histoire**, **beaucoup d'argent**
- **Conditional si**: only before *il* and *ils* $\rightarrow$ **s'il vient**, **s'ils veulent** (never elide before *elle*: write **si elle vient**).

#### 3. Hyphens in Inversions and Compound Nouns

The hyphen (*le trait d'union* `-`) is grammatically required in two primary TCF contexts:
1. **Formal Inversions in Task 2 and Task 3**:
   - **« Pourriez-vous me préciser les modalités ? »** — /pu.ʁje vu mə pʁe.si.ze le mɔ.da.li.te/ — *Could you clarify the terms for me?*
   - **« Peut-être devrions-nous reconsidérer ce projet. »** — /pø.t‿ɛtʁ də.vʁi.jɔ̃ nu ʁə.kɔ̃.si.de.ʁe sə pʁɔ.ʒɛ/ — *Perhaps we ought to reconsider this project.*
2. **Compound Civic Terms**:
   - **« Les États-Unis »** — /le.z‿e.ta.z‿y.ni/ — *The United States*
   - **« Le rond-point »** — /lə ʁɔ̃.pwɛ̃/ — *The roundabout*
   - **« Au-delà de »** — /o.də.la də/ — *Beyond*

### Ergonomic typing speed and the 30-WPM exam benchmark

In the TCF Expression écrite, you must produce:
- **Task 1**: 60 to 120 words (recommendation: 80–100 words).
- **Task 2**: 120 to 150 words (recommendation: 135–145 words).
- **Task 3**: 120 to 180 words (recommendation: 150–165 words).

Total output: **approx. 365 to 410 words**.
You have **60 total minutes** for all three tasks.

Let us examine the mathematical reality of your time budget:

| Activity | Time Allocation | Effective Velocity Required |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Reading prompts & planning outlines** | 12 minutes total | 0 words/min (analytical thinking) |
| **Active text drafting (375 words)** | 33 minutes total | **~11.5 composed words per minute** |
| **Systematic proofreading & error hunting** | 15 minutes total | 0 words/min (focused editing) |

While composing text at 11.5 words per minute sounds modest, remember that **composition speed is not raw typing speed**. When writing in a second language under examination anxiety:
- You pause to recall vocabulary and gender agreements.
- You hesitate over subjunctive conjugations and relative pronouns.
- You backspace to restructure sentences.

Empirical studies of computer-based language examinations show that candidates need a **raw mechanical typing speed of at least 25 to 35 words per minute (WPM)** in French to achieve an effective composing speed of 12 WPM. If your raw mechanical speed is only 15 WPM, drafting 375 words will consume 45 to 50 minutes, leaving you with zero time for outlining or proofreading.

#### Ergonomic Posture for High-Speed Typing

To prevent wrist fatigue and typing lockups during the 60-minute sprint:
1. **Home Row Anchor**: Rest your index fingers on `F` and `J` (look for the physical tactile bumps).
2. **Wrist Elevation**: Keep your wrists slightly elevated above the desk, not resting heavily on the desk edge or laptop bezel.
3. **Pinky Mastery for Dead Keys**: On US-International, use your right pinky for `'` and your left pinky for `Shift`. Do not take your hand off the home row to reach these keys.
