### Introduction

In the first section of Path 01, you mastered the **passé composé** to recount specific, completed past events and chronological actions. But when narrating past experiences in human conversation, you do not merely state what happened; you also paint the scenery, describe who was there, explain ongoing background circumstances, and recount past routines and childhood memories.

In French, this descriptive and habitual realm belongs to the **imparfait** (imperfect tense).

On the **TCF Canada examination**, understanding and producing the imparfait is an indispensable requirement for reaching CEFR Level A2 and breaking into Level B1:
- In **Listening (*Compréhension orale*)**, audio dialogues routinely set a scene using the imparfait (*« Il faisait froid ce matin-là... »*, *« Quand j'habitais à Sherbrooke... »*) before introducing a sudden event in the passé composé.
- In **Reading (*Compréhension écrite*)**, descriptive passages, autobiographical profiles, and travel testimonies rely on the imparfait to describe living conditions, weather, emotional states, and longstanding habits.
- In **Writing (*Expression écrite*) Task 2**, recounting an experience or comparing life before and after relocating to Canada requires contrasting past habits (*« Autrefois, je vivais dans une grande ville tropicale... »*) with present realities.
- In **Speaking (*Expression orale*) Task 1 and Task 3**, examiners will ask you about your background: *« Comment était votre vie dans votre pays d'origine ? »* (*What was your life like in your country of origin?*).

In this lesson, you will master the remarkably regular formula for conjugating the imparfait, conquer the single irregular stem in the entire language, and learn the three foundational communicative uses of this tense: physical/emotional descriptions, weather conditions, and repetitive past habits.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The two-step mechanical formula for forming the imparfait: the present tense **nous** stem + universal imparfait endings.
- The six invariant endings: **-ais**, **-ais**, **-ait**, **-ions**, **-iez**, and **-aient**.
- Auditory reality: why four of the six endings sound 100% identical in spoken French (/ɛ/).
- The single irregular verb stem in the entire language: the verb **être** (*ét-*).
- Core communicative uses: setting the background scene, physical appearance, and psychological states.
- Describing habitual, repetitive past actions with frequency markers (*autrefois, d'habitude, tous les jours*).
- Describing past weather and atmospheric conditions with *il faisait*, *il pleuvait*, and *il neigeait*.

### The mechanical formula for forming the imparfait

While the passé composé requires choosing between two auxiliaries and memorizing irregular past participles, the **imparfait is the most regular and predictable tense in the French language**.

To conjugate any verb in the imparfait (with only one single exception, the verb *être*), follow this invariable two-step formula:

```text
Step 1: Conjugate the verb with "nous" in the present tense, then drop "-ons" to find the stem.
Step 2: Add the universal imparfait endings (-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient).
```

#### Step 1: Finding the Imparfait Stem

Let us observe how dropping *-ons* from the present tense *nous* form produces the stem across regular and irregular verbs alike:

| Infinitive Verb | Present Tense with *Nous* | Drop *-ons* | Imparfait Stem |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **parler** | nous parl**ons** | -ons | **parl-** |
| **finir** | nous finiss**ons** | -ons | **finiss-** |
| **vendre** | nous vend**ons** | -ons | **vend-** |
| **faire** | nous fais**ons** | -ons | **fais-** (pronounced /fəz-/) |
| **avoir** | nous av**ons** | -ons | **av-** |
| **aller** | nous all**ons** | -ons | **all-** |
| **prendre** | nous pren**ons** | -ons | **pren-** |
| **boire** | nous buv**ons** | -ons | **buv-** |
| **vivre** | nous viv**ons** | -ons | **viv-** |

Notice that even notoriously irregular verbs like *faire*, *avoir*, and *boire* follow this rule with absolute precision!

#### Step 2: Adding the Universal Imparfait Endings

Regardless of verb group, the six endings are identical for every verb in the language:

| Subject Pronoun | Imparfait Ending | IPA Pronunciation | Example with *habiter* | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **J' / Je** | **-ais** | /ɛ/ | **J'habitais** | I used to live / was living |
| **Tu** | **-ais** | /ɛ/ | **Tu habitais** | You used to live / were living |
| **Il / Elle / On** | **-ait** | /ɛ/ | **Il habitait** | He used to live / was living |
| **Nous** | **-ions** | /jɔ̃/ | **Nous habitions** | We used to live / were living |
| **Vous** | **-iez** | /je/ | **Vous habitiez** | You used to live / were living |
| **Ils / Elles** | **-aient** | /ɛ/ | **Ils habitaient** | They used to live / were living |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Phonetic Reality: The /ɛ/ Homophone Block

Notice a crucial auditory fact: the endings for **je (-ais)**, **tu (-ais)**, **il/elle/on (-ait)**, and **ils/elles (-aient)** **all sound completely identical**: **/ɛ/** (open "e" as in English *bed* or French *fête*).

- *Je parlais* = /ʒə paʁ.lɛ/
- *Tu parlais* = /ty paʁ.lɛ/
- *Il parlait* = /il paʁ.lɛ/
- *Ils parlaient* = /il paʁ.lɛ/

In spoken French and TCF Listening, only the subject pronoun tells you who is performing the action. In written French, however, you must never confuse the singular *-ait* with the third-person plural ending **-aient**!

</div>

### The single irregular stem: the auxiliary verb etre

There is only **one verb** in the entire French language whose stem does not derive from the present tense *nous* form: the auxiliary verb **être** (because *nous sommes* does not end in *-ons*).

The stem for *être* in the imparfait is **ét-** (/et-/), to which you attach the standard endings:

| Subject Pronoun | Conjugated Form | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning | Example Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **J'** | **étais** | /e.tɛ/ | I was | *J'étais étudiant à l'université.* |
| **Tu** | **étais** | /e.tɛ/ | you were | *Tu étais toujours ponctuel.* |
| **Il / Elle / On** | **était** | /e.tɛ/ | he / she / one was | *L'appartement était très spacieux.* |
| **Nous** | **étions** | /e.tjɔ̃/ | we were | *Nous étions heureux de déménager.* |
| **Vous** | **étiez** | /e.tje/ | you were | *Vous étiez absents lors de la réunion.* |
| **Ils / Elles** | **étaient** | /e.tɛ/ | they were | *Les voisins étaient très accueillants.* |

Notice the mandatory liaisons with vowels:
- **Nous‿étions** (/nu.z‿e.tjɔ̃/)
- **Vous‿étiez** (/vu.z‿e.tje/)
- **Ils‿étaient** (/il.z‿e.tɛ/)
- **Elles‿étaient** (/ɛl.z‿e.tɛ/)

### Core communicative functions: descriptions, states, and weather

Unlike the passé composé, which reports actions with clear start and end points (*what happened*), the imparfait serves as the camera lens capturing the continuous background environment (*how things were*).

#### 1. Physical and Scenic Descriptions

Use the imparfait to describe the features of an apartment, a city, or a person in the past:

> **Notre premier logement à Montréal était un 4½ au deuxième étage.** — /nɔtʁə pʁə.mje lɔʒ.mɑ̃ a mɔ̃.ʁe.al e.tɛ œ̃ katʁ.e dmi o dø.zjɛm e.taʒ/  
> *Our first apartment in Montreal was a 4½ on the second floor.*

> **Le salon avait de grandes fenêtres et les chambres étaient lumineuses.** — /lə sa.lɔ̃ a.vɛ də ɡʁɑ̃d fə.nɛtʁ e le ʃɑ̃bʁ e.tɛ ly.mi.nøz/  
> *The living room had large windows and the bedrooms were bright.*

#### 2. Psychological, Emotional, and Physical States

Mental states, emotions, feelings, and physical sensations that lasted over an indefinite period take the imparfait:

| French Expression | IPA | English Meaning | Context Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **J'avais faim / soif** | /ʒ‿a.vɛ fɛ̃ / swaf/ | I was hungry / thirsty | *Après le voyage, nous avions très faim.* |
| **J'avais peur** | /ʒ‿a.vɛ pœʁ/ | I was afraid | *Au début, elle avait peur de l'hiver canadien.* |
| **Je voulais** | /ʒə vu.lɛ/ | I wanted | *Je voulais trouver un emploi rapidement.* |
| **Je savais** | /ʒə sa.vɛ/ | I knew | *Nous savions que le coût de la vie était élevé.* |
| **Je pensais** | /ʒə pɑ̃.sɛ/ | I thought | *Je pensais que la signature du bail était simple.* |

#### 3. Weather and Environmental Backdrop

Atmospheric conditions ongoing in the past are naturally expressed with the imparfait of impersonal verbs:

> **Il faisait très froid et il neigeait doucement.** — /il fə.zɛ tʁɛ fʁwa e il nɛ.ʒɛ dus.mɑ̃/  
> *It was very cold and it was snowing softly.*

> **Il y avait beaucoup de vent sur le pont Jacques-Cartier.** — /il j‿a.vɛ bo.ku də vɑ̃ syʁ lə pɔ̃ ʒak kaʁ.tje/  
> *There was a lot of wind on the Jacques Cartier Bridge.*

### Past habits, routines, and repeated actions

The second major communicative pillar of the imparfait is expressing **past habits** (*l'habitude dans le passé*)—actions that occurred repeatedly or continuously as part of an established routine, equivalent to English *"used to"* or *"would always"*.

When describing habits, sentences are frequently anchored by temporal frequency indicators:

| Frequency Marker | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning | Exemplary Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Autrefois** | /o.tʁə.fwa/ | In the past / Formerly | *Autrefois, nous habitions dans une maison.* |
| **À l'époque** | /a l‿e.pɔk/ | Back then / At that time | *À l'époque, je prenais le métro tous les matins.* |
| **D'habitude** | /d‿a.bi.tyd/ | Usually | *D'habitude, le propriétaire passait le samedi.* |
| **Tous les jours** | /tu le ʒuʁ/ | Every day | *Mon colocataire cuisinait tous les soirs.* |
| **Chaque semaine** | /ʃak səmɛn/ | Every week | *Nous visitions deux appartements chaque semaine.* |
| **Souvent** | /su.vɑ̃/ | Often | *En hiver, nous allions patiner au parc La Fontaine.* |

> **Quand j'étais adolescent, je jouais au soccer chaque fin de semaine.** — /kɑ̃ ʒ‿e.tɛ.z‿a.dɔ.lɛ.sɑ̃, ʒə ʒwɛ o sɔ.kœʁ ʃak fɛ̃ də səmɛn/  
> *When I was a teenager, I played soccer every weekend.*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In Québec spoken French, when people talk about past childhood routines or former lifestyles, you will often hear:
- **Dans l'temps** **(QC)** (/dɑ̃ l‿tɑ̃/): Colloquial Canadian equivalent of *autrefois* or *à l'époque*:
  > *« Dans l'temps, on n'avait pas l'air climatisé dans les logements. »* **(QC)**  
  > (Standard French: *« À l'époque, nous n'avions pas la climatisation dans les logements. »*)
- **Être habitué à** (/ɛtʁ a.bi.tɥe a/): Newcomers to Canada frequently discuss adapting to local climates:
  > *« Je n'étais pas habitué au froid intense de janvier. »* (/ʒə n‿e.tɛ pa.z‿a.bi.tɥe o fʁwa ɛ̃.tɑ̃s də ʒɑ̃.vje/)  
  > *I was not used to the intense cold of January.*

On the TCF Canada, using expressions like *« à l'époque »*, *« autrefois »*, and *« j'étais habitué à... »* demonstrates high lexical control on Speaking Task 1!

</div>

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--warning" markdown="1">

#### Exam tip: TCF Listening Trap (Passé Composé vs Imparfait)

TCF exam designers deliberately exploit the phonetic similarity between the **first-person passé composé** and the **imparfait** of regular *-er* verbs:

- Passé composé: **J'ai parlé** (/ʒ‿e paʁ.le/) → Notice the closed **/e/** sound at the end. Indicates a single completed event (*I spoke*).
- Imparfait: **Je parlais** (/ʒə paʁ.lɛ/) → Notice the two syllables with an open **/ɛ/** at the end. Indicates an ongoing state or habit (*I used to speak / was speaking*).

During the 10-second preview window for Listening items, check whether the question asks about a **general condition in the past** (*Quelle était la situation ?*) or a **specific decision** (*Qu'a fait la personne ?*). Training your ear to distinguish /e/ from /ɛ/ is essential for securing Upper A2 listening points!

</div>
