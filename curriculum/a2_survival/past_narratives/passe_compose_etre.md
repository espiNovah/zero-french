### Introduction

In the previous lesson, you conquered the mechanics of the **passé composé with avoir**, which covers the vast majority of French verbs. You learned how to form regular and irregular past participles, place negation and adverbs correctly, and formulate questions.

Now, we tackle the remaining minority—the verbs that use **être** (/ɛtʁ/, to be) as their auxiliary. While relatively small in number, verbs that conjugate with *être* represent some of the highest-frequency, most indispensable communicative actions in the French language: arriving, leaving, going, coming, staying, moving, and the entire spectrum of reflexive daily routine actions (*waking up, getting dressed, preparing oneself*).

On the **TCF Canada examination**, mastering the passé composé with *être* is a non-negotiable benchmark:
- In **Writing (*Expression écrite*) Task 1 and Task 2**, recounting an immigration journey, travel itinerary, weekend getaway, or daily routine requires verbs like *aller*, *partir*, *arriver*, *s'installer*, and *se lever*.
- In writing, **past participle agreement with the grammatical subject** is a visible marker of grammatical control. Forgetting the feminine *-e* or plural *-s* on participles conjugated with *être* is one of the most frequent written errors at this level, and examiners do assess grammatical accuracy.
- In **Listening (*Compréhension orale*)**, recognizing the auxiliary forms of *être* (*je suis allé*, *il est parti*, *ils sont arrivés*) allows you to immediately identify completed physical movements, arrivals, and departures in transportation announcements and personal narratives.

In this lesson, you will master the two categories of verbs that conjugate with *être*, memorize the classic **DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP** movement verbs, navigate reflexive verbs in the past, internalize the golden rule of subject agreement, and understand the subtle difference when dual-auxiliary verbs switch to *avoir*.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Dual-auxiliary verbs (*monter, descendre, sortir, rentrer, passer, retourner*): when a direct object forces a switch from *être* to *avoir*.

### The two kingdoms of auxiliary etre: movement verbs and reflexive verbs

In English, compound tenses always use the verb "to have" (*I have arrived*, *I have washed myself*). In Old and Modern French, however, the auxiliary **être** is reserved for two distinct semantic kingdoms:

1. **Intransitive Verbs of Movement and Change of State**: Verbs that describe a person moving their entire body from one location to another, or undergoing an elemental transition of existence (birth, death, transformation). These verbs do not take a direct object; the subject is the entity moving or transforming.
2. **All Reflexive and Reciprocal Verbs (*les verbes pronominaux*)**: Any verb accompanied by a reflexive pronoun (*me, te, se, nous, vous*) where the subject performs the action upon itself (*se réveiller*, *se laver*) or mutually with others (*se téléphoner*, *se rencontrer*). (They all take *être*, but their participle agreement follows a different rule: see the exception below.)

Let us review the present tense of the auxiliary **être**, which serves as the grammatical backbone for these verbs:

| Subject Pronoun | Auxiliary (Être) | IPA Pronunciation | Example with *partir* | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Je / J'** | **suis** | /sɥi/ | **Je suis parti(e).** | I left / I have left. |
| **Tu** | **es** | /ɛ/ | **Tu es parti(e).** | You left / You have left. |
| **Il** | **est** | /ɛ/ | **Il est parti.** | He left / He has left. |
| **Elle** | **est** | /ɛ/ | **Elle est partie.** | She left / She has left. |
| **On** | **est** | /ɛ/ | **On est parti(s).** | We / One left. |
| **Nous** | **sommes** | /sɔm/ | **Nous sommes parti(e)s.** | We left / We have left. |
| **Vous** | **êtes** | /ɛt/ | **Vous êtes parti(e)(s).** | You left / You have left. |
| **Ils** | **sont** | /sɔ̃/ | **Ils sont partis.** | They (m.) left / They have left. |
| **Elles** | **sont** | /sɔ̃/ | **Elles sont parties.** | They (f.) left / They have left. |

Notice the liaisons with auxiliary **être**:
- **Vous‿êtes parti(e)s** (/vu.z‿ɛt paʁ.ti/) — mandatory liaison between pronoun *vous* and vowel *êtes* (/z/).
- **Ils sont‿arrivés** (/il sɔ̃.t‿a.ʁi.ve/) — mandatory liaison between auxiliary *sont* and vowel *arrivés* (/t/).
- **Elles sont‿arrivées** (/ɛl sɔ̃.t‿a.ʁi.ve/) — mandatory liaison between auxiliary *sont* and vowel *arrivées* (/t/).
*(Note: There is no liaison in "Nous sommes partis" or "Ils sont partis" because "sommes" and "partis" begin with consonants).*

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--critical" markdown="1">

#### Critical Meaning Trap: "Rester" Does NOT Mean "To Rest"

The verb **rester** (/ʁɛs.te/) is a classic false friend (*faux ami*). It does **not** mean "to rest" (which is *se reposer* in French). **Rester** means **to stay** or **to remain**:
- **Je suis resté au bureau** = *I stayed at the office.*
- **Je me suis reposé au bureau** = *I rested at the office.*

Furthermore, even though *rester* denotes staying still rather than dynamic movement, it always conjugates with **être**!

</div>

### Reflexive verbs in the compound past: daily routines and actions

All reflexive and reciprocal verbs (*les verbes pronominaux*)—verbs preceded by the reflexive pronoun **se** in their infinitive form—**exclusively use être** in the passé composé.

#### 1. Affirmative Sentence Word Order

The reflexive pronoun sits immediately in front of the conjugated auxiliary verb **être**:

```text
[Subject] + [Reflexive Pronoun] + [Auxiliary Être] + [Past Participle]
```

Let us observe the complete paradigm of **se lever** (/sə lə.ve/, to get up; *to wake up* is **se réveiller**):

| Person | French Sentence | IPA Pronunciation | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Je** | **Je me suis levé(e)** | /ʒə mə sɥi lə.ve/ | I got up |
| **Tu** | **Tu t'es levé(e)** | /ty t‿ɛ lə.ve/ | You got up |
| **Il** | **Il s'est levé** | /il s‿ɛ lə.ve/ | He got up |
| **Elle** | **Elle s'est levée** | /ɛl s‿ɛ lə.ve/ | She got up |
| **On** | **On s'est levé(e)s** | /ɔ̃ s‿ɛ lə.ve/ | We / One got up |
| **Nous** | **Nous nous sommes levé(e)s** | /nu nu sɔm lə.ve/ | We got up |
| **Vous** | **Vous vous êtes levé(e)(s)** | /vu vu.z‿ɛt lə.ve/ | You got up |
| **Ils** | **Ils se sont levés** | /il sə sɔ̃ lə.ve/ | They (m.) got up |
| **Elles** | **Elles se sont levées** | /ɛl sə sɔ̃ lə.ve/ | They (f.) got up |

Notice the elision before vowels: *te* becomes **t'** (*tu t'es réveillé*), and *se* becomes **s'** (*il s'est installé*).

#### 2. Negative Sentence Word Order with Reflexive Verbs

When negating a reflexive verb in the compound past, the **ne** (or **n'**) precedes the reflexive pronoun, and **pas** follows the auxiliary verb **être**:

```text
[Subject] + ne/n' + [Reflexive Pronoun] + [Auxiliary Être] + pas + [Past Participle]
```

> **Je ne me suis pas réveillé à l'heure.** — /ʒə nə mə sɥi pa ʁe.vɛ.je a l‿œʁ/  
> *I did not wake up on time.*

> **Elle ne s'est pas inquiétée pour les démarches.** — /ɛl nə s‿ɛ pa.z‿ɛ̃.kje.te puʁ le de.maʁʃ/  
> *She did not worry about the administrative procedures.*

> **Nous ne nous sommes pas perdus dans le centre-ville.** — /nu nə nu sɔm pa pɛʁ.dy dɑ̃ lə sɑ̃.tʁə.vil/  
> *We did not get lost downtown.*

### The golden rule of agreement: matching the subject

When a verb is conjugated with the auxiliary **être**, the past participle behaves like an adjective: **it must agree in gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural) with the grammatical subject**. (This rule covers the movement verbs. Pronominal verbs usually look as if they follow it too, but they actually follow a different rule, explained in the exception below.)

```markdown
1. Masculine Singular: base form (no addition)
2. Feminine Singular: add -e
3. Masculine Plural: add -s
4. Feminine Plural: add -es
```

Let us examine how this agreement rule manifests across different subjects:

| Subject | Gender & Number | Example with *aller* | Example with *se préparer* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Thomas** | Masc. Sing. | Thomas est **allé** | Thomas s'est **préparé** |
| **Camille** | Fem. Sing. | Camille est **allée** (+e) | Camille s'est **préparée** (+e) |
| **Thomas et Marc** | Masc. Plur. | Ils sont **allés** (+s) | Ils se sont **préparés** (+s) |
| **Camille et Sarah** | Fem. Plur. | Elles sont **allées** (+es) | Elles se sont **préparées** (+es) |
| **Thomas et Camille** | Mixed Plur. | Ils sont **allés** (+s) | Ils se sont **préparés** (+s) |

#### The Auditory vs Orthographic Reality

In spoken French, the masculine form (*allé*), the feminine form (*allée*), the masculine plural (*allés*), and the feminine plural (*allées*) **sound 100% identical**: /a.le/.

However, on the **computer-based TCF Canada Writing test**, you type your text yourself; do not count on any spell-checker to fix agreements. Examiners assess grammatical accuracy, and a missing agreement on verbs taking *être* is an easy error for them to spot.

#### Exception: Reflexive Verbs with a Direct Object

For pronominal verbs, the participle does not really agree with the subject: **it agrees with the direct object when that direct object comes before the verb**. In most cases the reflexive pronoun *se* is that direct object and refers to the subject, so the agreement looks like subject agreement (*Elle s'est levée*, *Elles se sont rencontrées*: she raised herself, they met each other). There are two cases with **no agreement**:

1. **A direct object noun follows the verb**, so *se* is only an indirect object.
2. **The verb itself takes an indirect object** (*téléphoner à, parler à, dire à, demander à, sourire à*), so *se* is indirect: **Elles se sont téléphoné.** **Ils se sont parlé.** (no *-es*, no *-s*).

Compare these two sentences carefully:
- **Elle s'est lavée.** (*She washed herself.* → Who did she wash? Herself (*s'* is the direct object). The participle agrees with *elle* → **-ée**).
- **Elle s'est lavé les mains.** (*She washed her hands.* → What did she wash? *Les mains* (direct object follows the verb). The participle remains uninflected → **-é**).

> **Elle s'est brossé les dents.** (No agreement because *les dents* follows).  
> **Ils se sont acheté une nouvelle voiture.** (No agreement because *une nouvelle voiture* follows).

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--warning" markdown="1">

#### Exam tip

On **TCF Writing Task 2 (120–150 words)**, you are frequently asked to write an article, a blog post, or a letter recounting a personal experience (e.g. *« Racontez une journée mémorable lors d'un voyage »* or *« Partagez votre expérience d'intégration au Canada »*).

Candidates who write in the first person (*je*) must maintain internal gender consistency:
- If you establish a female authorial voice in your introduction (*« En tant que nouvelle résidente... »*), every subsequent past participle with *être* must carry the feminine agreement: *« Je suis arrivée au Québec en plein hiver. Je me suis installée dans un petit appartement... »*.
- If you write about yourself and your family using *nous* or *on*, remember that *nous* takes plural agreement (*« Nous sommes partis tôt »*). If you use informal *on* to mean "we", the participle takes a plural agreement: *« On est arrivés à l'aéroport »*.

Inconsistent participle agreement across a 150-word text is a visible accuracy error that examiners will notice!

</div>

### Dual-auxiliary verbs: when movement verbs take avoir

Six of the verbs in the DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP family are capable of taking either **être** or **avoir**, depending on whether they are used **intransitively** (no direct object) or **transitively** (with a direct object):

```markdown
The 6 Dual-Auxiliary Verbs:
Monter · Descendre · Sortir · Rentrer · Retourner · Passer
```

1. **Intransitive (Movement of Subject alone) → Auxiliary ÊTRE**:
   - The subject moves from one location to another. There is no direct object receiving the action. The past participle agrees with the subject:
   > **Je suis monté au deuxième étage.** — /ʒə sɥi mɔ̃.te o dø.zjɛm e.taʒ/  
   > *I went up to the second floor.* (Movement of self)

   > **Elle est sortie de la maison.** — /ɛ.l‿ɛ sɔʁ.ti də la mɛ.zɔ̃/  
   > *She went out of the house.* (Movement of self)

2. **Transitive (Action performed ON an object) → Auxiliary AVOIR**:
   - The subject is moving or taking an external physical object. The verb is followed directly by a direct object noun (*les valises, le chien, la poubelle*). The auxiliary switches to **avoir**, and the participle **does not agree with the subject**:
   > **J'ai monté les valises au deuxième étage.** — /ʒ‿e mɔ̃.te le va.liz o dø.zjɛm e.taʒ/  
   > *I carried the suitcases up to the second floor.* (Transitive: suitcases = direct object)

   > **Elle a sorti le chien.** — /ɛ.l‿a sɔʁ.ti lə ʃjɛ̃/  
   > *She took the dog out.* (Transitive: dog = direct object)

| Verb | Intransitive with Être (Subject moves) | Transitive with Avoir (Object moved) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **descendre** | *Il est descendu du bus.* (He got off the bus.) | *Il a descendu les cartons.* (He carried the boxes down.) |
| **sortir** | *Nous sommes sortis hier.* (We went out yesterday.) | *Nous avons sorti nos passeports.* (We took out our passports.) |
| **rentrer** | *Tu es rentré tard.* (You came home late.) | *Tu as rentré la voiture au garage.* (You pulled the car into the garage.) |
| **retourner** | *Elle est retournée à Paris.* (She returned to Paris.) | *Elle a retourné la crêpe.* (She flipped the crepe.) |
| **passer** | *Je suis passé par Toronto.* (I passed through Toronto.) | *J'ai passé un examen.* (I took / sat a test; to pass it is *réussir un examen*.) |

<div class="lesson-note lesson-note--tip" markdown="1">

#### Québec vs France

In Québec, the arrival and settlement experience is central to immigration discourse. When discussing moving into a new apartment or home, Canadian Francophones frequently use the verb **emménager** (/ɑ̃.me.na.ʒe/, to move in) and **s'installer** (/s‿ɛ̃s.ta.le/, to settle in):

> **Nous nous sommes installés à Gatineau il y a trois ans.** — /nu nu sɔm.z‿ɛ̃s.ta.le a ɡa.ti.no il j‿a tʁwa.z‿ɑ̃/  
> *We settled in Gatineau three years ago.*

Note that while *déménager* (to move out / relocate) conjugates with **avoir** (*J'ai déménagé*), *s'installer* is reflexive and therefore always conjugates with **être** (*Je me suis installé(e)*).

On the TCF Canada exam, using verbs like *s'installer*, *s'adapter*, and *s'intégrer* in past narrations shows authentic command of Canadian immigration vocabulary.
